# Tutor — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `tutor` · **Aliases:** learning specialist, test-prep instructor
**Also read:** `teacher`, `teaching-assistant`, `substitute-teacher`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Tutor, Private Tutor, Subject Tutor (math, reading, science, writing), Online Tutor, Test-Prep Instructor, Academic Coach, and Learning Specialist
- Adjacent worth searching: Reading Interventionist, After-School Program Instructor, Writing Center or College Learning Center Tutor, Teaching Assistant, and Instructional Aide

## Where the postings live
- Usually tutoring centers, online tutoring platforms, school districts and colleges (for learning centers and intervention programs), and the general job boards
- Many private tutors find clients through word of mouth, parent groups, schools, and local listings rather than job postings, and online platforms often hire through a quick application and a subject test

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Subject knowledge you can show (a degree, courses, test scores, or teaching experience), and the grade levels you can tutor
- A background check, often including a check for work with children; school-based roles may ask for a paraprofessional or substitute credential depending on the state
- Availability, usually after school, evenings, and weekends, and comfort with online tools for virtual sessions

## Story types that land
- Helping a student go from struggling to confident in a subject, with an outcome from your own record
- Adapting your approach for a student who learned differently
- Keeping parents informed and motivated
- Building a test-prep plan and sticking to it

## Resume conventions for this profession
- One page; list subjects and grade levels, number of students or sessions from your own record, and any credentials or training
- "Passionate educator" reads as filler; "tutored middle-school math weekly online, built practice sets, reported progress to parents" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a short interview plus a subject test or a mock tutoring session
- Many centers and platforms add a background check, and schools may use a panel interview

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $20.84 an hour ($43,350 a year); most earn between $14.15 and $36.53 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 25-3041). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 34,200 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to shrink 0.2% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** some college, without a degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 25-3041). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
- **Self-employed:** about 12% of people in this work are self-employed, and the pay figures above leave them out (BLS Projections 2025–35).
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly or per session; private tutors set their own rates, while centers and platforms commonly pay a set rate per session or per hour
- Shift differentials are uncommon in tutoring; rates more often differ by subject or test prep
- Employee tutors at centers are usually non-exempt, so overtime pay applies after a full workweek under federal law, but self-employed and independent-contractor tutors usually don't get overtime
- Guaranteed hours are rare; sessions often depend on student demand, so ask about minimum hours and how cancellations are paid

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open: your rate for advanced subjects or test prep, cancellation terms, travel time, and session length
- Private tutors may set their own rates and policies, but they tend to be set against local rates for similar tutoring

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: pay only for completed sessions with unpaid prep time, contractor status for set hours in a center, and fees to join a platform

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state education department for school-based roles; the Department of Labor's pages on employees versus independent contractors; the platform's or center's pay terms
