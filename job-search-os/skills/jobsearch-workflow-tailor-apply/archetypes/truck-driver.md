# CDL Truck Driver — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `truck-driver` · **Aliases:** OTR driver, CDL-A driver, regional/local driver
**Also read:** `delivery-driver`, `logistics-supply-chain`, `warehouse-supervisor`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- CDL-A Driver, Tractor-Trailer Driver, Over-the-Road (OTR) Driver, Regional Driver, Local or Home-Daily Driver, Line Haul Driver, Dedicated Route Driver, Tanker, Flatbed, or Refrigerated Driver, and Team Driver
- Adjacent worth searching: Driver Trainer or Driver Mentor, Yard Jockey or Shuttle Driver, CDL-B roles (dump truck, straight truck, bus), Dispatcher, Driver Manager or Fleet Manager, and Safety or Recruiting Coordinator at a carrier

## Where the postings live
- Usually carrier career pages, trucking-specific job boards, the general job boards, and driving schools, which often have hiring partners; private fleets (companies that haul their own goods) tend to post on their own career sites
- Many carriers recruit continuously and move fast by phone; recruiters commonly call within a day of an online application, and referrals from current drivers often come with a bonus for the referrer

## What a recruiter screens for first
- A commercial driver's licence (CDL) of the right class, with the endorsements the freight needs (hazardous materials, tanker, doubles and triples) and a current Department of Transportation (DOT) medical card
- For new drivers, the licensing path in plain words: a commercial learner's permit (CLP) first, then federal Entry-Level Driver Training (ELDT) from a school or carrier listed on the FMCSA Training Provider Registry, then the state skills test; hazardous materials endorsements also need a background check
- Your driving record and safety history (moving violations and accidents show up on your motor vehicle record; failed or refused drug and alcohol tests and other drug and alcohol violations show up in the federal Drug and Alcohol Clearinghouse), recent verifiable experience, and the freight type and home time you want
- Your age: driving a truck across state lines usually requires being at least 21 (federal driver qualification rules), so interstate carriers commonly screen for it first; some intrastate roles accept younger drivers under state rules

## Story types that land
- A trip where weather, a breakdown, or a tight appointment went wrong and how you kept the load safe and the customer informed
- A clean safety and inspection record you can point to, and the habits behind it (pre-trip inspections, following distance, planning around hours-of-service limits)
- Handling a tough dock, a tight backing situation, or a load problem (shifted freight, reefer temperature, paperwork mismatch)
- On-time delivery and good communication with dispatch and customers over a long stretch

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Many carriers use an online driver application rather than a resume; when a resume is asked for, put CDL class, endorsements, state, and medical card near the top, then each job with freight type, equipment, route type (OTR, regional, local), and safety record
- "Safe and reliable driver" reads as filler; "regional flatbed, securement of steel and lumber, no preventable accidents, clean roadside inspections" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter phone call, then an online application that pulls your motor vehicle record, work history, and Clearinghouse check, then orientation where a road test, drug screen, and physical commonly happen
- Many carriers hire at orientation rather than through a formal interview; expect questions about home time, the freight you've hauled, and any gaps or incidents on your record

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $28.19 an hour ($58,640 a year); most earn between $19.30 and $38.17 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 53-3032). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 214,500 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 3.8% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a certificate or diploma program (not a degree), the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 53-3032). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Commonly paid per mile for long-haul and regional work (ask whether the mileage is practical route miles or shorter "household" miles), hourly for many local, yard, and private-fleet jobs, and a percentage of the load for many owner-operators and some company drivers
- Extra pay commonly comes as detention (waiting time), stop pay, layover, breakdown pay, and extra rates for hazardous materials, team runs, or night work where the carrier pays a differential; safety, fuel, and on-time bonuses are common
- Many drivers fall under the federal motor carrier overtime exemption, so overtime after a set number of hours often doesn't apply, especially on interstate runs; some local and small-vehicle work and some state laws differ, so ask how overtime is handled
- Some carriers offer guaranteed hours or a weekly pay guarantee for new drivers or slow weeks; ask what triggers it and for how long
- Owner-operators and lease drivers pay their own fuel, insurance, maintenance, and truck payments, so compare net income after expenses, not gross revenue

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open: your starting per-mile rate or hourly rate (by verifiable experience), home-time schedule, dedicated lanes, equipment assignment, and sign-on bonus timing
- Company-paid CDL training commonly comes with a contract to stay for a period or repay the cost; ask for the repayment terms in writing, since they tend to be firmer than the pay

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: "up to" per-mile rates or weekly pay with no typical figure, lease-purchase deals where the carrier controls your loads and your truck payment, training contracts with steep repayment, unpaid detention, pressure to run past hours-of-service limits, and vague home-time promises

## Verify before relying on this
- The FMCSA site for ELDT, medical certification, and hours-of-service rules; your state driver licensing agency for CDL steps and fees; the Department of Labor's motor carrier exemption fact sheet for overtime; the carrier's written pay plan before signing
