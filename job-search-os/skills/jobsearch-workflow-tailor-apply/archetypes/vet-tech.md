# Veterinary Technician / Assistant — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `vet-tech` · **Aliases:** vet tech, CVT, veterinary assistant
**Also read:** `medical-assistant`, `dental-assistant`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Veterinary Technician with the state's credential (Certified, Licensed, or Registered Veterinary Technician: CVT, LVT, RVT), Veterinary Nurse, Emergency or Surgical Veterinary Technician, Veterinary Assistant, and lead or head technician roles
- Adjacent worth searching: specialty technician in internal medicine, anesthesia, dentistry or critical care, research animal technician, shelter medicine roles, and technical or sales roles with veterinary suppliers

## Where the postings live
- Usually general and specialty animal hospitals, emergency clinics, shelters, research facilities, and the general job boards; state veterinary technician associations and school job boards also carry postings
- Many clinics hire through referrals and externships, so the practice where you did your clinical rotation is often the first place to ask

## What a recruiter screens for first
- For technician roles, the state credential in most states: usually graduation from a veterinary technology program accredited by the American Veterinary Medical Association, a passing score on the Veterinary Technician National Examination (run by the American Association of Veterinary State Boards), and state registration or licensure; veterinary assistant roles usually don't carry a state credential
- Hands-on skills the posting names: anesthesia monitoring, catheter placement, dental cleaning, radiographs, lab work, and restraint; plus experience with the clinic's practice-management software
- Emergency and specialty hospitals commonly screen for night, weekend and holiday availability

## Story types that land
- Catching a change in a patient under anesthesia or in recovery and alerting the veterinarian in time
- Keeping a busy appointment day moving: triage, restraint, lab work and client handoffs
- Explaining home care clearly to a worried owner
- Handling controlled-drug logs or inventory accurately

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Credential type, state and number near the top, then the practice type (general, emergency, specialty, shelter), species you work with, and the procedures you perform independently
- "Animal lover" reads as filler on its own; "anesthesia monitoring for daily surgeries, dental radiographs, IV catheters in cats and dogs" reads as credible (use your own details)

## How interviews usually run
- Often a phone screen, then an interview with the practice manager and head technician, commonly followed by a working interview or shadow shift where you handle patients
- Expect questions on anesthesia monitoring, restraint safety, handling euthanasia appointments, and dealing with upset clients

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $18.34 an hour ($38,150 a year); most earn between $14.48 and $23.63 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 31-9096, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 36,600 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 2 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a high school diploma or equivalent, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 31-9096). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly, with shift differentials for overnight and weekend work at emergency hospitals; overtime after a full work week is common in busy practices, so ask how often it happens and how it's scheduled
- Common extras: pet-care discounts, uniform allowances, continuing-education money, and credential or licence fee reimbursement; credentialed technicians usually earn more than assistants

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open to discussion: the hourly rate (credential and specialty skills tend to move it), differentials, guaranteed hours or a set schedule, continuing-education money, and a raise tied to earning the credential
- Ask whether the practice pays for the national exam or specialty certification study

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: "tech" roles that don't use your credential, assistants expected to do credentialed tasks the state reserves for technicians, frequent unscheduled overtime, and working interviews that run long without pay

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state veterinary medical board for credential titles and what technicians and assistants may legally do; the American Association of Veterinary State Boards (aavsb.org) for the national exam; the American Veterinary Medical Association for accredited programs
