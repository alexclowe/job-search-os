# Construction Manager / Superintendent — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `construction-manager` · **Aliases:** project manager (construction), superintendent, foreman
**Also read:** `project-manager`, `civil-engineer`, `carpenter`, `safety-specialist`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Construction Manager, Project Manager (construction), Superintendent or General Superintendent, Assistant Superintendent, Foreman or General Foreman, Site Manager, Project Engineer (the common entry route), and Assistant Project Manager
- Adjacent worth searching: Preconstruction Manager, Estimator, Owner's Representative, Facilities Project Manager, Scheduler, and Construction Safety Manager

## Where the postings live
- Usually general contractor and builder career pages, construction-specific job boards, the general boards, and recruiters who specialize in construction; public agencies and owners post owner-side roles on their own sites
- Many superintendent and foreman roles are filled by word of mouth, by subcontractors and suppliers vouching for someone, or by following a superintendent from job to job

## What a recruiter screens for first
- The project types and sizes you've run (commercial, residential, heavy civil, health care), your role on them, and the scheduling and project management software you use (such as Procore, Microsoft Project, or Primavera P6)
- A construction management or engineering degree for many project manager tracks, while superintendents and foremen often come up through the trades; an OSHA 30-hour card is commonly asked for, and certifications such as Certified Construction Manager (CCM) or PMP may help
- Some states licence general contractors or require a qualifying individual on the licence; ask whether the role needs you to hold or qualify one

## Story types that land
- A job that fell behind schedule or over budget and how you got it back
- A safety incident or near miss and what you changed on site
- Managing subcontractors: a conflict, a poor performer, or a coordination problem between trades
- Handling an owner or architect change, a request for information, or a claim without a dispute

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Lead with a project list (type, size or value from your own records, your role, delivery method such as design-build), then certifications and software; mark any figure you can't confirm and fill it in before sending
- "Oversaw all aspects of construction" reads as filler; "ran site for a four-story medical office, coordinated a dozen trades, closed out punch list ahead of the owner's move-in" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a phone screen, then an interview with the operations manager or a senior project manager, sometimes a jobsite visit; larger firms may add a panel with safety or preconstruction leaders
- Expect scenario questions about a delay, a failed inspection, a subcontractor dispute, or a change order, and questions on reading a schedule and managing cost

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $79,920 a year ($38.42 an hour); most earn between $53,280 and $128,260 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 47-1011, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 116,700 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 2 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a high school diploma or equivalent, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 47-1011). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
- **Self-employed:** about 11% of people in this work are self-employed, and the pay figures above leave them out (BLS Projections 2025–35).
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually an annual salary, with a year-end or project-completion bonus common at many builders; a truck or vehicle allowance, fuel card, and phone are common benefits for field roles; foremen and some field supervisors are paid hourly, often under a union scale, so ask how overtime is paid
- Some foremen and general foremen are paid hourly under the trade's union scale rather than salary; travel and per diem often apply on out-of-town projects

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open: base salary, bonus structure and how it's tied to project results, vehicle allowance, per diem and travel, title, and the size or type of the next project you'd run
- Ask how bonuses have actually paid out and whether they depend on the company's results as well as your project's

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: one person expected to run several large sites at once, "project manager" roles that are mostly estimating or admin, no mention of safety support, heavy travel with no per diem, and bonuses described as discretionary with no history

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state contractor licensing board (CareerOneStop's License Finder lists them; link, don't copy); OSHA for the construction outreach training program; the certifying body's own site for CCM or PMP requirements

## In Canada
<!-- canada:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **NOC 2021:** 70010; 72014, matched by title by the maintainers (not an official concordance; check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site).
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- Construction manager and superintendent titles are not usually licensed, but residential builders need a provincial licence or registration in some provinces, and industry certifications and safety training requirements vary; check your province's builder licensing and occupational health and safety rules.
- "Engineer" is a protected title in every province and territory (Alberta exempts only the title "software engineer"; per Engineers Canada and APEGA), so using a project engineer title without a provincial engineering licence can breach the law, and Canadian postings often use another title for this role (per Engineers Canada); check your provincial engineering regulator before using it.
