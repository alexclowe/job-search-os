# Welder — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `welder` · **Aliases:** fabricator, welder-fitter
**Also read:** `manufacturing-worker`, `maintenance-technician`, `plumber`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Welder, Welder Helper, MIG (metal inert gas) Welder, TIG (tungsten inert gas) Welder, Stick or Structural Welder, Pipe Welder, Fabrication Welder, Welder-Fitter or Fitter-Welder, Maintenance Welder, and Lead Welder
- Adjacent worth searching: Fabricator, Structural Steel Fitter, Robotic Welding Operator or Technician, Boilermaker, Pipefitter, Ironworker, Welding Inspector, and Welding Instructor

## Where the postings live
- Usually fabrication shops, manufacturers, shipyards, energy and pipeline contractors, and staffing agencies for skilled trades, plus the general job boards; union welders in the pipe and boilermaker trades are commonly dispatched through the local union
- Many shops hire after a weld test on site, and community college and trade-school programs often feed entry roles through instructor referrals

## What a recruiter screens for first
- The processes and positions you can weld (MIG, TIG, stick, flux-core; flat, vertical, overhead; plate or pipe), the metals you know, and whether you can read blueprints and weld symbols
- Code-based qualifications that commonly matter: a weld test to a structural or pressure-piping code, AWS (American Welding Society) certifications, or an employer's own test; qualifications often lapse if you stop welding in that process, so keep your continuity records
- Fit-up skills, measuring and layout, and safety training such as hot-work and confined-space awareness

## Story types that land
- A weld test or a code job you passed, and the process and position it covered
- A fabrication you built from prints that fit the first time
- A quality problem you caught (distortion, porosity, a bad fit-up) before it shipped
- Working safely in a hot, tight, or elevated space, or training a newer welder

## Resume conventions for this profession
- A skills block near the top by process, position, and material, then qualifications and the code each covers, then each job with the shop type and what you built
- "Expert welder in all processes" reads as filler; "TIG and stick on carbon and stainless pipe, all positions, qualified to the shop's pressure-piping procedure" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a short interview with the shop foreman, then a hands-on weld test that is cut and bend-tested or visually inspected; some employers pay for the test time and some don't
- Expect questions on the processes and positions you hold, reading prints, and the metals and thicknesses you have worked on

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $25.84 an hour ($53,750 a year); most earn between $18.87 and $37.28 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 51-4121). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 40,300 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 2.4% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a high school diploma or equivalent, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 51-4121). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly, with shift differentials on second and third shifts at manufacturers; overtime is common on production deadlines and field jobs, and federal and state rules usually set when it is owed
- Field and pipeline work may add per-diem or travel pay, and some shops pay more for each added qualification; ask about guaranteed hours on project work, where jobs can end suddenly

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open: the starting rate (commonly set after the weld test), shift assignment, pay for added qualifications, per-diem on travel jobs, and paid training or test fees
- Ask whether the employer pays for your qualification tests and how often retests happen

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: an unpaid weld test that runs for hours, "must provide all own equipment" including the welder, no mention of fume control or ventilation, and the same role re-posted every few weeks

## Verify before relying on this
- The American Welding Society (aws.org) for certification programs; the posting itself for the code and procedure you will be tested to; the welding, cutting, and brazing pages of OSHA (the Occupational Safety and Health Administration) for safety rules; the Department of Labor's apprenticeship site for registered programs
