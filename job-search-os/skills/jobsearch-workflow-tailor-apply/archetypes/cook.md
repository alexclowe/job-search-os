# Cook / Line Cook / Chef — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `cook` · **Aliases:** line cook, prep cook, sous chef, head chef
**Also read:** `restaurant-front-of-house`, `food-service-manager`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Line Cook, Prep Cook, Grill or Fry Cook, Breakfast Cook, Banquet Cook, Short-Order Cook, Institutional or Cafeteria Cook, Sous Chef, Chef de Partie, Kitchen Manager, and Head Chef or Executive Chef
- Adjacent worth searching: cook roles in hospitals, schools, senior living, and corporate dining (often steadier hours), Catering Cook, Commissary or Production Cook, and Private or Personal Chef

## Where the postings live
- Usually the general job boards, hospitality-specific job boards and apps, restaurant sites with quick-apply forms, and culinary school job boards; institutional kitchens often post on hospital, school district, or contract food-service career pages
- Many kitchen hires happen through referrals from other cooks and chefs, and walking in to ask for the chef or kitchen manager outside service hours often works

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Which stations you can run (grill, sauté, fry, prep, pantry), the volume and style of kitchen you've worked in, and your availability for nights, weekends, and holidays
- A food handler card where the state or county asks for one; kitchen managers and chefs are commonly expected to hold a food protection manager certification
- For chef roles, menu costing, ordering, inventory, and running a team; culinary school helps but experience usually counts more

## Story types that land
- Holding your station together during a heavy rush with tickets coming out right
- Keeping food safe: temperature logs, labeling and rotating stock, passing a health inspection
- Cutting waste or food cost, or fixing a prep system that kept running short
- For chefs, building a menu, training a line, or fixing a kitchen that kept falling behind

## Resume conventions for this profession
- One page, often through a quick-apply form or handed to the chef in person; for each kitchen, name its style and volume, your stations, and certifications
- "Passionate about food" reads as filler; "sauté and grill on a busy dinner line, prepped for the next shift, passed every health inspection" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a short conversation with the chef or kitchen manager, then a working interview or "stage" where you cook a shift or a dish; ask whether that trial shift is paid
- Chef roles may add a tasting, a menu-costing exercise, or questions about running a team and hitting food-cost targets

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $17.98 an hour ($37,390 a year); most earn between $13.80 and $23.03 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 35-2014, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 546,200 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 6 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** no formal credential, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 35-2014). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly for cooks; sous chefs, kitchen managers, and head chefs are often paid an annual salary, sometimes with a bonus tied to food cost or sales
- Overtime is common in busy kitchens; hourly cooks usually earn overtime after a full workweek, but some salaried chef roles are classified as exempt, so ask how overtime is handled before accepting
- Some kitchens pay a differential for overnight or early-morning shifts, and some kitchens share tips with cooks through a tip pool, which federal rules allow only when the employer pays at least the full federal minimum wage (DOL Fact Sheet #15)
- Guaranteed hours are uncommon; ask how hours are cut in slow seasons, and whether unionized kitchens (common in hotels and some institutions) publish a wage schedule; some cities have scheduling laws that require advance notice or pay for last-minute changes

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open: your hourly rate by station experience, schedule and days off, and for chefs the salary, bonus terms, and staff meals or paid time off
- Institutional and union kitchens usually place you on a set scale, so the step you start on tends to matter more than the rate itself

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: salaried roles with "flexible hours" that may mean very long weeks without overtime, long unpaid trial shifts, chef titles that are really line cook jobs, and constant reposting of the same opening

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state or county health department for food handler and food protection manager rules; the Department of Labor's overtime and tipped-employee pages, and your state labor department, for overtime and tip pool rules
