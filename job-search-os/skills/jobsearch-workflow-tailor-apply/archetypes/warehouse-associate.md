# Warehouse Associate / Material Handler — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `warehouse-associate` · **Aliases:** stocker, order picker, packer, forklift operator
**Also read:** `inventory-shipping-clerk`, `warehouse-supervisor`, `delivery-driver`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Warehouse Associate, Warehouse Worker, Material Handler, Order Picker or Order Selector, Packer, Loader or Unloader, Stocker, Receiving Associate, and Forklift Operator or Reach Truck Operator
- Adjacent worth searching: Shipping and Receiving Clerk, Inventory Control Associate, Returns Processor, Yard Driver, Warehouse Lead or Team Lead, and Distribution Center Associate

## Where the postings live
- Usually the general job boards, company career pages with a quick-apply form, and staffing agencies; many large distribution centers post the same opening continuously and hire in waves before busy seasons
- Temp-to-hire through a staffing agency is common, and walk-in hiring events or on-the-spot offers happen often, so applying in person or calling the agency can move faster than waiting on an online form

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Whether you can work the posted shift (nights, weekends, overtime in peak season), reliable transport to the site, and whether you can do the physical work described in the posting, such as lifting, standing, and working in cold or heat
- Forklift, reach truck, or pallet jack experience; in the US, OSHA (the federal workplace safety agency) has the employer train and evaluate each operator on its own equipment, so a "forklift certification" from a past job helps but usually gets redone at the new site
- Experience with handheld scanners and a warehouse management system (WMS), plus a clean safety record; many employers also run a background check and a drug screen

## Story types that land
- Hitting pick or pack rates accurately without cutting safety corners
- Catching a mislabeled pallet, a count mismatch, or damaged freight before it shipped
- Covering extra shifts or learning a new area during peak season
- Spotting a safety hazard (blocked aisle, bad racking, a near-miss) and reporting it

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Keep it to one page, and often the online form is the whole application; list each job with the site type (distribution center, cold storage, retail back room), the equipment you ran, and any rate or accuracy results from your own record
- "Hard worker" reads as filler; "order picker on nights, RF scanner and electric pallet jack, trained on reach truck, no safety incidents" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a short phone screen or none at all, then an in-person interview or hiring event that may include a site tour, a short video on the work, and questions about attendance, shift preference, and safety
- Some employers add a skills check on equipment, a drug screen, or a background check before a start date; offers tend to come quickly, sometimes the same day

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $19.35 an hour ($40,240 a year); most earn between $15.03 and $26.51 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 53-7062, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 897,700 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 4 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** no formal credential, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 53-7062). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly, with shift differentials for nights, weekends, or cold-storage work at many sites; some sites add attendance, safety, or productivity incentives
- Overtime is common in peak season; hourly warehouse workers are usually non-exempt, so overtime pay for long weeks commonly applies under federal law, and some states add daily overtime rules
- Guaranteed hours are uncommon; ask how hours are cut when volume drops ("voluntary time off" or sending people home early) and whether the site offers any minimum weekly hours
- Agency temp work often pays a different rate from direct hire, and unionized warehouses usually publish wage steps in the contract

## What's usually negotiable
- The starting rate often moves little at entry level, but shift choice, differential eligibility, and the agency's conversion date to direct hire may be open, especially with equipment experience
- Where a union scale exists, placement on the scale by experience usually matters more than the rate itself

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: "must be able to work mandatory overtime" with no limit stated, productivity quotas the employer won't explain (some states have laws requiring warehouse quota disclosure), temp roles "with potential to hire" that rarely seem to convert, and fees charged by an agency to start

## Verify before relying on this
- OSHA's powered industrial trucks pages for forklift training rules; the Department of Labor's overtime pages and your state labor department for overtime and quota laws; the posting and offer letter for the shift, rate, and differentials
