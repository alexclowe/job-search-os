# Interior Designer — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `interior-designer` · **Aliases:** interior decorator, space planner, commercial interior designer, kitchen and bath designer
**Also read:** `construction-manager`, `graphic-designer`, `project-manager`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Interior Designer, Junior or Senior Interior Designer, Commercial or Contract Interior Designer, Residential Interior Designer, Health Care or Hospitality Interior Designer, Space Planner, Kitchen and Bath Designer, Design Assistant, and Project Designer
- Adjacent worth searching: Interior Design Coordinator, Furniture or Workplace Designer at a dealer, Design Consultant in retail showrooms, Color and Materials Designer, Visual Merchandiser, Facilities or Workplace Planner, and Interior Decorator (which usually doesn't need licensing)

## Where the postings live
- Employed roles usually post on architecture and design firm sites, furniture dealer and manufacturer sites, the general and creative job boards, and professional association job boards
- Many designers are self-employed, and residential clients commonly come from referrals, a portfolio website, social media, contractors, and showrooms; many firms also hire from student portfolio reviews and internships

## What a recruiter screens for first
- A portfolio showing the project types in the posting (commercial, residential, health care, hospitality), with drawings and finished photos; software such as AutoCAD, Revit, SketchUp, and Adobe tools
- The credential path in plain words: a degree from an accredited interior design program is common, and many designers then take the NCIDQ exam from the Council for Interior Design Qualification (CIDQ), currently given in three sections (IDFX, IDPX, and IDIX), after the education and work experience it asks for
- State rules: many states regulate interior design through a title law (only qualified people may call themselves a registered or certified interior designer), and a smaller number also regulate the practice itself, especially for commercial work; ask whether the role needs you to be registered or eligible

## Story types that land
- A space plan that solved a real problem for the people using it, and how you got there
- Keeping a project on budget and schedule while working with contractors, vendors, and the client
- Coordinating code and accessibility requirements with architects or engineers
- A client who kept changing their mind and how you kept the project moving

## Resume conventions for this profession
- The portfolio leads; the resume lists education, NCIDQ status or state registration, software, and each role with project types, sizes, and your part in them
- "Creative visionary" reads as filler; "produced construction documents in Revit for office fit-outs, specified finishes and furniture, and ran site walks with the contractor" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a portfolio review with a principal or design director, then an interview with the studio team; some firms add a design exercise or a software test
- Expect questions on your role in portfolio projects, working to a budget, handling client changes, and codes and accessibility

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $67,190 a year ($32.31 an hour); most earn between $41,420 and $114,140 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 27-1025). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 7,700 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 2.7% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 27-1025). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
- **Self-employed:** about 26% of people in this work are self-employed, and the pay figures above leave them out (BLS Projections 2025–35).
<!-- facts:end -->
- Employed designers are commonly paid an annual salary at firms, while assistants, showroom designers, and some junior roles are hourly; some retail and showroom roles add commission on product sales
- Hourly designers usually earn overtime under federal and state law during deadline weeks, while salaried designers may be exempt; ask how the role is classified and whether hourly roles come with guaranteed hours
- Self-employed designers commonly charge a flat design fee, an hourly rate, a percentage of the project cost, or a markup on furniture and materials; compare that income after expenses and slow months against a salaried role with benefits

## What's usually negotiable
- Employed roles often have room on salary within the band, title, support for NCIDQ exam fees and study time, continuing education, and remote or hybrid days
- Freelance work: the fee structure, retainer or deposit, number of revision rounds, and payment schedule tend to move; ask for them in a written contract

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: "interior designer" roles that are mostly sales quotas, unpaid design tests that look like real client work, freelance offers without a signed contract or deposit, and senior titles with junior pay or no drawing software named

## Verify before relying on this
- CIDQ (cidq.org) for the NCIDQ exam and eligibility; your state interior design board or licensing office for title and practice rules (CareerOneStop's License Finder lists them; link, don't copy)

- Only some provinces protect the title: in Ontario, for example, the title interior designer is reserved by provincial law for registered members of ARIDO, and several provinces' associations ask for the NCIDQ exam from CIDQ, the same one used in the US; check your provincial interior design association before using the title.
