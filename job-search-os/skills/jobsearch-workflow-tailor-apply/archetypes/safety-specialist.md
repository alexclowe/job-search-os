# Occupational Health & Safety Specialist — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `safety-specialist` · **Aliases:** EHS specialist, safety coordinator
**Also read:** `compliance-officer`, `production-supervisor`, `construction-manager`, `industrial-engineer`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Safety Specialist, Safety Coordinator, EHS (environment, health, and safety) Specialist or Coordinator, Safety Officer, Health and Safety Advisor, Industrial Hygienist, and EHS Manager or Safety Manager at the senior end
- Adjacent worth searching: Site Safety Manager on construction projects, Risk Control or Loss Control Consultant for insurers, Environmental Compliance Specialist, Ergonomist, Safety Trainer, and Workplace Safety Inspector roles in government

## Where the postings live
- Usually manufacturer, construction, energy, logistics, hospital, and university career pages, insurer listings for loss-control roles, government job portals, and the general job boards; professional safety association job boards commonly carry specialist roles
- Construction safety roles often fill through general contractors and staffing firms that place site safety staff project by project

## What a recruiter screens for first
- The industry and hazards you know (construction, general industry, chemical, healthcare), the programs you have run (incident investigation, training, audits, lockout/tagout, respiratory protection), and your knowledge of the rules of OSHA (the Occupational Safety and Health Administration) for that industry
- Credentials that commonly help, from the BCSP (Board of Certified Safety Professionals): the ASP (Associate Safety Professional) and CSP (Certified Safety Professional), and technician-level credentials such as the CHST (Construction Health and Safety Technician) for construction; OSHA 10- or 30-hour cards are course completion cards, not certifications
- A degree in occupational safety or a related science is often preferred for specialist roles, and site roles commonly screen for travel and early-morning availability

## Story types that land
- An incident investigation: what happened, the root cause you found, and the fix that kept it from recurring
- A program you built or turned around, with a leading or lagging result you can explain
- Getting supervisors and crews to follow a rule because they understood why
- Preparing for or handling a regulatory inspection

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Credentials near the top, then each role with the industry, sites or headcount covered, the programs owned, and one or two results; list the regulations and standards you work to in plain words
- "Passionate safety champion" reads as filler; "ran incident investigations and monthly audits across two plants, rebuilt the lockout/tagout program, cut recordable injuries" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter screen, then interviews with the EHS or operations manager and site leaders, commonly with scenario questions such as a worker refusing unsafe work or a supervisor pushing back on a rule
- Some employers ask you to review a job hazard analysis, walk a site and point out hazards, or present a short training

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $90,150 a year ($43.34 an hour); most earn between $55,000 and $134,950 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 19-5011, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 20,900 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 2 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 19-5011). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually an annual salary; some employers add a bonus tied to company or safety goals, and construction or field roles may add travel or per-diem pay
- Coordinator-level roles are sometimes paid hourly and eligible for overtime, so ask how the role is classified

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open: base salary within the band, paid certification exams and continuing education, a vehicle or travel allowance for multi-site roles, remote or office days, and start date
- Ask whether the safety bonus is tied to low injury counts, since that can discourage reporting

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: one person covering many sites with no support, a role that reports to the person whose output it polices with no way to escalate, bonuses tied to injury-free counts, and "safety" roles that are mostly clerical

## Verify before relying on this
- BCSP (bcsp.org) for credential eligibility; OSHA (osha.gov) for current standards and what its training cards do and don't mean; your state's workplace safety plan where one applies
