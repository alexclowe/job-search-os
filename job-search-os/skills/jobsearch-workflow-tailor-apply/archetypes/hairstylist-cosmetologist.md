# Hairstylist / Cosmetologist / Esthetician — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `hairstylist-cosmetologist` · **Aliases:** hairdresser, barber, nail tech, esthetician
**Also read:** `personal-trainer`, `retail-sales-associate`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Hairstylist, Hair Stylist, Cosmetologist, Hairdresser, Colorist, Barber, Esthetician, Nail Technician, Lash Technician, and senior, master, or educator levels at salons that use a tiered price ladder
- Adjacent worth searching: salon or spa manager, platform or brand educator for a product line, beauty-school instructor, salon front-desk or guest-services lead, and theatrical or bridal makeup work

## Where the postings live
- Usually salon and spa career pages, the general job boards, and beauty-school job boards; many salons hire through stylist referrals and their own social media
- Booth and suite rentals are often advertised by the salon or suite owner directly rather than on job boards, and some chains recruit students before they finish school

## What a recruiter screens for first
- An active state licence for the work you'll do: usually hours at a state-approved school, then a written and a practical exam (many states use the national exams from the National-Interstate Council of State Boards of Cosmetology), then the licence; barbering, esthetics and nail work are often separate licences
- A portfolio of your work (often on social media), the services you're strong in (colour, cuts, extensions, skin, nails), and whether you'd bring clients with you
- A practical skills check or model day, and your availability for evenings and weekends

## Story types that land
- Building a clientele from zero: how you filled your chair and kept people rebooking
- A colour correction or tricky request you handled, including telling a client what wasn't realistic
- Growing service tickets through add-ons or retail products without pushing
- Keeping a station clean and safe, and handling an unhappy client well

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Licence type and state near the top, then the services you specialise in, product lines you're trained on, and any continuing-education classes; a portfolio link usually matters more than the resume itself
- "Passionate about beauty" reads as filler; "colour specialist, blondes and corrections, most clients on a regular rebooking cycle" reads as credible (use your own details)

## How interviews usually run
- Often a short conversation with the salon owner or manager, then a practical or "model day" where you cut or colour a model while they watch
- Commonly asked: how you'd build a book of clients, how you handle a client who hates the result, and what the salon's education and apprenticeship path looks like for newer stylists

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $35,790 a year ($17.21 an hour); most earn between $27,040 and $71,190 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 39-5012, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 109,500 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 3 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a certificate or diploma program (not a degree), the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 39-5012). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
- **Self-employed:** about 48% of people in this work are self-employed, and the pay figures above leave them out (BLS Projections 2025–35).
<!-- facts:end -->
- Pay commonly follows one of three models: commission on services (often with a smaller commission on retail products), hourly pay plus tips, or booth rent where you rent a chair or suite and keep what you charge
- Some commission salons pay an hourly guarantee or a commission, whichever is higher, while you build clients; ask whether that guarantee is recoverable from later commission or non-recoverable
- Ask whether commission is reduced or charged back when a service is refunded or redone, and whether product or supply fees (sometimes called back-bar fees) come out of your commission
- Overtime rules for commission-paid salon staff can differ from ordinary hourly rules, so ask how overtime is handled; booth renters are usually self-employed and pay their own taxes, supplies and insurance

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open to discussion: the commission split or hourly rate, the tier or level you start at, guaranteed hours or a pay guarantee while you build a clientele, education and product discounts, and your station and schedule
- Ask who keeps the clients if you leave: many salons treat the client list as theirs and use non-solicit clauses, so read that language before signing

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: "unlimited earning potential" with no base or guarantee, product-sales quotas tied to your pay, fees for education you're expected to take, heavy non-compete language, and booth rent that looks like a job (set hours, dress code, salon pricing) without employee protections

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state board of cosmetology (or barbering) for licence types, school hours and reciprocity, or CareerOneStop's License Finder; the salon's written pay plan and any non-compete before you sign; the IRS and your state tax agency if you rent a booth

- Job Bank lists hairstylist trade certification as compulsory in Nova Scotia, Ontario, Manitoba, Saskatchewan, and Alberta, New Brunswick requires a licence from the Cosmetology Association of New Brunswick to use the hairstylist or hairdresser title, and certification is voluntary in most other places, with a Red Seal endorsement available through the interprovincial exam; esthetics and nail rules differ by province and city, so check Job Bank's requirements for your province before relying on a US licence.
