# Nurse — job-search archetype

**Slug:** `nurse` · **Aliases:** RN, registered nurse, staff nurse, charge nurse (RN)
**Also read:** `licensed-practical-nurse`, `nurse-practitioner`, `nursing-assistant-caregiver`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Staff Nurse / Registered Nurse (unit named: med-surg, ICU, ED, L&D, peds, oncology, periop), Charge Nurse, Nurse Manager, Clinical Nurse Specialist, Nurse Educator, Case Manager, Utilization Review Nurse, Infection Prevention Nurse, Informatics Nurse
- Adjacent worth searching: Clinical Documentation Specialist, Quality/Patient Safety Nurse, Telehealth/Triage Nurse, Occupational Health Nurse, Legal Nurse Consultant, Clinical Research Nurse

## Where the postings live
- Health-system career sites (most volume), hospital association boards, specialty-society job boards, travel/contract agencies for short assignments, staffing agencies, and the general boards
- Many unit openings are filled through internal transfer or referral before they are posted externally; a recruiter contact at the system matters

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Active licence in the state (or compact licence), BLS/ACLS/PALS or the unit's required certifications, specialty certification where the unit expects it, degree level (many acute-care systems prefer or require a BSN), and years in the *same* unit type
- EHR experience by name (Epic, Cerner/Oracle Health, Meditech) is often a filter; shift and unit fit is asked before skills

## Story types that land
- A patient-safety catch: what you noticed, what you did, what it prevented
- Handoff, throughput, or documentation improvements on your unit (a process you changed, not a metric you invented)
- Precepting, charge, or committee work that shows scope beyond your assignment
- A hard family conversation or de-escalation handled well

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Licence and certifications near the top with numbers/expiry where the employer expects them; unit type, bed count, and patient population per role; one page for early career, two for experienced
- "Provided compassionate care" reads as filler; "12-bed ICU, 1:2 ratio, vents and drips" reads as credible. Keep the specifics; drop the adjectives

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter screen, then a panel with the nurse manager and staff nurses; scenario and behavioral questions ("tell me about a time a patient's condition changed") are common, and some units add a shadow shift or a unit tour
- Outpatient and clinic roles tend to ask more about patient education, phone triage, care coordination, and the scheduling or charting system

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $46.90 an hour ($97,550 a year); most earn between $33.14 and $66.09 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 29-1141). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 180,800 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 5.6% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 29-1141). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Many RNs start with an associate degree (ADN); some employers, especially hospitals, prefer or require a BSN
- Usually hourly, with differentials for nights, weekends, charge, or specialty; overtime rules, on-call and call-back pay, and guaranteed hours matter as much as the base rate
- Leadership and non-bedside roles (nurse manager, case manager, educator) are more often paid an annual salary
- Many systems place new hires on an internal step or clinical-ladder scale by years of experience; unionized hospitals publish their wage steps in the contract

## What's usually negotiable
- Where a published scale exists, the base rate usually follows it; placement on the scale (years credited), schedule, shift, sign-on bonus and its repayment terms, relocation, and start date tend to move more easily
- Continuing-education support and certification pay are worth asking about

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: "Must be flexible with floating" plus no unit named, unrealistic ratios stated as a positive, sign-on bonus with an unusually long clawback, "self-scheduling" paired with mandatory overtime language

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state board of nursing and the NCSBN for licence and compact status; the certifying body (ANCC, AACN, etc.) for credential rules; the employer's own posting for unit requirements

## In Canada

- **NOC 2021:** 31301, matched by title by the maintainers (not an official concordance; check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site).
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- Registered nurses are regulated by each province's or territory's nursing regulator, and a US licence doesn't carry over; internationally educated nurses usually start with the National Nursing Assessment Service (outside Quebec). Many hospital roles are unionized, with wage grids in the collective agreement. Rules differ by province, so check your provincial regulator first.
