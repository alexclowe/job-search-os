# Social Worker — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `social-worker` · **Aliases:** LCSW, LMSW, case manager
**Also read:** `mental-health-counselor`, `human-services-assistant`, `school-counselor`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Social Worker with the setting named: Child Welfare or Child Protective Services Worker, Foster Care or Adoption Social Worker, School Social Worker, Medical or Hospital Social Worker, Hospice Social Worker, Clinical Social Worker or Therapist (LCSW), Mental Health or Substance Use Social Worker, and Case Manager
- Adjacent worth searching: Care Coordinator, Discharge Planner, Family Service Worker, Program Coordinator or Director at a nonprofit, Victim Advocate, Community Health Worker, and Utilization Review or Behavioral Health roles at health plans

## Where the postings live
- Usually state and county government job sites (child welfare and public assistance), hospital and health-system career pages, school district HR sites, nonprofit job boards, and the general job boards
- Many agencies hire through civil-service lists or exams; field placements during a social work degree often turn into job offers

## What a recruiter screens for first
- The degree: a Bachelor of Social Work (BSW) or Master of Social Work (MSW) from an accredited program, and for clinical roles the state clinical licence (often called LCSW) or eligibility for it
- The licensing path in plain words: licensing is by state, commonly with levels tied to degree and supervised experience, and most states use exams from the Association of Social Work Boards (ASWB), which offers bachelors, masters, advanced generalist, and clinical exams; clinical licences usually need supervised post-master's practice first
- Setting experience (child welfare, medical, school, mental health), caseload size, documentation systems, a valid driver's licence for home visits, and background checks

## Story types that land
- A family or client in crisis and how you assessed safety and coordinated services
- A discharge or transition plan that held because of your follow-up
- Advocating for a client with another agency, school, or insurer
- Keeping documentation accurate and timely on a heavy caseload, and how you protect against burnout

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Degree and licence (with state and number as the employer asks) near the top, then each role with setting, population, caseload size, and the services you gave; include field placements early in a career
- "Made a difference in clients' lives" reads as filler; "carried a caseload of foster families, ran monthly home visits and court reports, and coordinated school and health services" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a panel interview with a supervisor and team members; government roles commonly use structured scored questions, and some add a written exercise such as a case note or a short assessment write-up
- Expect questions on a safety decision, ethics and boundaries, handling a client who is angry or disengaged, documentation, and self-care

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $59,550 a year ($28.63 an hour); most earn between $42,280 and $95,530 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 21-1021, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 69,100 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 4 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 21-1021). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually an annual salary; public agencies and many hospitals pay from published pay grades or union scales with steps, and some health and hospice roles pay hourly or per visit, where it's worth asking about overtime eligibility and how many visits a day the role expects
- Clinical licensure commonly moves pay up; ask whether the employer provides or pays for clinical supervision toward licensure, which can be worth a lot

## What's usually negotiable
- In government and union roles, the pay grade tends to be fixed, but step placement for prior experience may move; in nonprofits and health care, base salary, caseload, and schedule are often more open
- Ask about paid clinical supervision, licence and continuing education costs, mileage reimbursement for home visits, and eligibility for public service loan forgiveness where it applies

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: caseloads the posting won't describe, "must be available after hours" with no on-call pay, clinical titles at a non-clinical rate, no supervision offered for new graduates, and the same role re-posted often

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state social work licensing board for licence levels and supervision rules; the Association of Social Work Boards (aswb.org) for exams; the Council on Social Work Education for accredited programs; CareerOneStop's License Finder for state links

- Social work is regulated provincially, and in most provinces only registered members of the provincial social work regulator may use the title social worker; some provinces, for example British Columbia and Alberta, use the ASWB licensing exams also used by US states (Alberta for several application pathways), so check your provincial social work regulator for registration steps before relying on a US licence.
