# Training & Development Specialist — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `training-specialist` · **Aliases:** corporate trainer, L&D specialist
**Also read:** `instructional-designer`, `hr-specialist-recruiter`, `hr-manager`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Training Specialist, Corporate Trainer, Learning and Development Specialist, Training Coordinator, Technical or Software Trainer, Sales Trainer, Leadership Development Specialist, Onboarding Specialist, and senior or manager versions
- Adjacent worth searching: instructional designer, enablement specialist, talent development partner, safety trainer, and customer education specialist

## Where the postings live
- Usually company career pages and the general boards, often under HR or operations; industry-specific trainer roles (health care, manufacturing, software) are commonly posted by those departments rather than HR
- Many trainers move in from the frontline job they used to train others on, so internal postings and referrals often matter

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Evidence you've run training: audiences, formats (classroom, virtual, on-the-job), and results; subject knowledge in the area you'll train, such as a system, product, or safety program
- Tools such as a learning management system, virtual-classroom software, and slide or authoring tools; professional certifications, such as those from the Association for Talent Development, are usually optional but can help

## Story types that land
- A training program that changed a measure: faster onboarding, fewer errors, better scores, or fewer safety incidents
- Handling a tough room: skeptical staff, a mixed skill level, or a failed demo
- Building training from scratch when nothing existed
- Getting managers to back training so it stuck after the session

## Resume conventions for this profession
- A skills line with delivery formats and tools, then roles with who you trained, how many in words or from your records, and what changed afterward
- "Dynamic facilitator" reads as filler; "ran new-hire system training for customer-service staff and rebuilt it as virtual sessions" reads as credible (use your own details)

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter screen, a hiring-manager interview, then a short teach-back or mock training session in front of a panel
- Expect questions on measuring training results, adapting to different learners, and working with subject experts and managers

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $69,280 a year ($33.31 an hour); most earn between $38,760 and $123,250 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 13-1151). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 46,000 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 10.8% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 13-1151). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually an annual salary; some employers add a bonus, and technical and sales trainers sometimes earn more than general trainers
- Contract trainers are often paid hourly or per day, and travel-heavy roles may include travel pay or per-diem expenses

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open to discussion: base salary, title, travel amount and how it's paid, remote days, certification or conference budget, and the tools you'll get
- Ask how the role's success is measured, which often shapes the bonus if there is one

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: a trainer who also owns all HR administration, heavy travel with no mention of travel pay, no budget for tools, and training roles set up mainly to fix a hiring or turnover problem

## Verify before relying on this
- The employer's posting for tools and audiences; the Association for Talent Development (td.org) for certification details if you're considering one; the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook for the field's outlook

## In Canada

- **NOC 2021:** possibly 11200. This is an uncertain title match by the maintainers, not an official concordance, so check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site first.
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- Training and development work is usually unregulated in Canada, and HR designations are optional; some employers value an HR designation from CPHR Canada, HRPA in Ontario, or the Ordre des CRHA in Quebec, so check the posting and Job Bank's requirements.
