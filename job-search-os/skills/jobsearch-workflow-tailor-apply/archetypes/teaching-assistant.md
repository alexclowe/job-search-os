# Teaching Assistant / Paraprofessional — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `teaching-assistant` · **Aliases:** paraeducator, instructional aide
**Also read:** `teacher`, `early-childhood-educator`, `substitute-teacher`, `tutor`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Teaching Assistant, Teacher Aide, Instructional Assistant, Paraprofessional or Paraeducator, Educational Assistant, Classroom Aide, and Special Education Paraprofessional (sometimes called a one-to-one aide or behaviour support aide)
- Adjacent worth searching: Substitute Teacher, Behavior Technician (such as a Registered Behavior Technician in autism services), Before- and After-School Program Staff, Preschool Assistant Teacher, Library or Media Aide, and Tutor or Interventionist roles

## Where the postings live
- Usually school district HR pages and application systems, state or regional education job boards, charter and private school sites, and the general boards; special education and behaviour-support agencies also place aides in schools
- Hiring tends to peak before the school year and again mid-year as needs change; many districts keep a standing application open and call from it

## What a recruiter screens for first
- For instructional roles in schools that receive federal Title I funding, the federal paraprofessional standard: a high school diploma plus either at least two years of college study, an associate degree or higher, or a passing score on a state or local assessment such as the ParaPro; many states add their own permit or certificate
- Background check and fingerprinting, CPR and first aid for some roles, experience with the age group or with students with disabilities, and for special education roles, comfort with personal care, behaviour plans, and safe physical support where trained

## Story types that land
- Helping a student who was stuck with a skill, following the teacher's plan, and what changed
- Calming a student in distress or following a behaviour plan consistently, and how you reported it
- Supporting a student with a disability to join in the regular class
- Working smoothly with a lead teacher, including when you disagreed about an approach

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Short, one page is common; list the paraprofessional qualification or state permit, training (such as crisis prevention or behaviour technician training), grade levels, and the kinds of support you gave (small reading groups, one-to-one, life skills)
- "Assisted the teacher" reads as filler; "ran daily small-group phonics for five second-graders using the teacher's plan and logged progress" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often one interview with the principal, the lead teacher, or a special education coordinator; some districts screen first with a short online questionnaire or group interview
- Expect scenario questions: a student refusing work, a student who gets physical, a parent who asks you about another child, and how you'd handle disagreeing with the teacher

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $36,780 a year; most earn between $27,150 and $50,040 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 25-9045). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 176,200 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to shrink 0.3% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** some college, without a degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 25-9045). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly, paid for the school days and hours worked, though some districts pay an annual salary spread across the year from a published classified-staff salary schedule with steps for years of service
- Ask how summers and school breaks work: many aides aren't paid for days school is closed, so ask whether pay is spread over the year or only on work days; guaranteed hours can shift if a student you support moves or leaves
- Overtime rules follow federal and state law for hourly staff and tend to come up for field trips, bus duty, or after-school programs; shift differentials are uncommon in schools, though some districts add pay for special education, bilingual, or behaviour-support roles

## What's usually negotiable
- The salary schedule itself rarely moves; step placement for prior experience, the classification (general aide vs special education or behaviour support), and hours per day may be worth asking about
- Many districts offer tuition help or pathways into teaching; ask whether one exists and whether it comes with a commitment to stay

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: one-to-one roles with little mention of training, "other duties as assigned" covering lunchroom, bus, and recess every day, hours set just under the benefits cutoff, and repeated re-posting of the same role, which may signal turnover or a hard assignment

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state department of education for paraprofessional permits and the assessment it accepts; the district's HR page for the classified salary schedule and union contract; the U.S. Department of Education for Title I rules
