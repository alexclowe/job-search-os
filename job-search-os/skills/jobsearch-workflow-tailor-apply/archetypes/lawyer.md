# Lawyer / Attorney — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `lawyer` · **Aliases:** associate attorney, in-house counsel
**Also read:** `paralegal`, `compliance-officer`, `management-consultant`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Associate or Attorney (junior, mid-level, senior), Counsel, Of Counsel, Staff Attorney, Assistant General Counsel, Corporate Counsel, General Counsel, Prosecutor, Public Defender, and Law Clerk (judicial clerkship)
- Adjacent worth searching: Contract Attorney or Document Review Attorney, Legal Operations roles, Compliance Counsel, Policy Counsel, and law-adjacent roles such as contracts manager where a law licence helps but isn't the whole job

## Where the postings live
- Usually firm and company career pages, legal-specific job boards, bar association boards, and law school career offices; government roles commonly post on government job sites
- Many firm roles, especially lateral moves, run through legal recruiters, and new-graduate hiring often comes through structured on-campus or summer programs rather than open postings

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Bar admission in the right state and good standing: the usual path is a law degree, the bar exam (many states use the Uniform Bar Exam from the NCBE, which says "Between July 2026 and July 2028, most jurisdictions will transition to the NextGen UBE"), a professional responsibility exam, and a character and fitness review; some states admit lawyers from other states on motion, so check the state's bar admission office
- Practice area and years of experience (class year at firms), the kind of matters handled, and, for firm roles, a portable book of business or billable hours history from your own record
- Law school and grades weigh heavily for new graduates at some employers and far less for experienced lawyers

## Story types that land
- A matter you drove: the problem, your analysis, what you advised or argued, and the result for the client
- A deal or contract you negotiated and the risk you spotted before signing
- Explaining a legal risk to a non-lawyer so they could make a decision
- Managing a heavy docket or several deadlines without anything slipping

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Bar admissions and education near the top (with honors and law review where relevant); for each role, the practice area and representative matters described without breaking client confidences
- Experienced lawyers often add a deal or case list; "handled complex litigation" reads as filler, while "drafted and argued summary judgment motions in commercial contract disputes" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a screening interview, then a round of back-to-back interviews with partners or in-house leaders; lateral candidates commonly discuss their book and practice fit
- Many employers ask for a writing sample, and some in-house or government roles use a timed written exercise or a panel

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $159,670 a year ($76.76 an hour); most earn between $78,360 and $351,600 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 23-1011). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 28,700 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 4.7% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a doctoral or professional degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 23-1011). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
- **Self-employed:** about 11% of people in this work are self-employed, and the pay figures above leave them out (BLS Projections 2025–35).
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually an annual salary; at many firms, associate pay follows a scale by class year, and bonuses are commonly tied to meeting billable-hour targets plus quality or merit
- Some firms pay lockstep by class year while others pay more on merit, so ask how the firm sets pay and bonuses and whether billable targets include pro bono or training hours
- In-house roles tend to pay a salary with a yearly bonus and fewer billable expectations; government and public-interest roles usually follow a published pay scale and may qualify for loan forgiveness programs
- Partners and lawyers with their own clients may be paid a share of profits or origination credit, which is a different arrangement from associate salary

## What's usually negotiable
- For lateral moves, class-year credit and title often move more than salary within a lockstep scale; sign-on bonuses, bar and relocation costs, and the billable target may also be negotiable
- In-house, the salary, bonus target, title, and remote days tend to move; ask what happens to your origination credit and clients if you leave a firm

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: a very high billable target with no mention of bonus, "sole attorney" roles covering every legal area for a large company, contract roles described as permanent, and pay well below the local scale for the class year asked for

## Verify before relying on this
- The state bar or the state's board of law examiners for admission rules; the NCBE (ncbex.org) for exam formats and which states use which exam; the employer's published pay scale where one exists

## In Canada
<!-- canada:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **NOC 2021:** 41101, matched by title by the maintainers (not an official concordance; check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site).
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- Lawyers are licensed by provincial law societies (for example the Law Society of Ontario), usually after a law degree, licensing exams, and articling or a practical program, and lawyers trained outside Canada often start with an assessment of their degree by the Federation of Law Societies of Canada's National Committee on Accreditation (NCA) before the provincial steps; Quebec runs its own civil law system, so check your provincial law society.
