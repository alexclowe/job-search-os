# HR Specialist / Recruiter — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `hr-specialist-recruiter` · **Aliases:** recruiter, talent acquisition, HR generalist, benefits specialist, HR coordinator
**Also read:** `hr-manager`, `training-specialist`, `administrative-assistant`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- HR Specialist, HR Generalist, HR Coordinator, HR Representative, HR Analyst, Recruiter, Corporate Recruiter, Talent Acquisition Specialist, Benefits Specialist, and Employment Representative
- Adjacent worth searching: Sourcer, Recruiting Coordinator, People Operations Specialist, HR Business Partner (usually more senior), Compensation or Benefits Analyst, HRIS (HR Information Systems) Analyst, Personnel Officer in government, and Agency or Staffing Recruiter

## Where the postings live
- Usually company career pages, the general job boards, and professional networks; HR associations and local chapters often run job boards, and government personnel roles are posted on government job sites
- Staffing and recruiting agencies hire recruiters continuously, and many in-house roles are filled through referrals from HR peers

## What a recruiter screens for first
- The HR areas you've handled (recruiting, onboarding, benefits, employee relations, payroll inputs, compliance) and for recruiters, the kinds of roles you've filled and your hiring volume
- Systems such as Workday or another HRIS, an applicant tracking system (ATS), and Excel; knowledge of employment law basics; no licence is usually needed, though many employers value a voluntary certification such as the SHRM-CP (from the Society for Human Resource Management) or the PHR (from HRCI)

## Story types that land
- Filling a hard-to-fill role or building a pipeline that shortened time to hire
- Handling a sensitive employee-relations issue fairly and within policy
- Improving onboarding, benefits enrollment, or an HR process so fewer things fell through the cracks
- Keeping the company compliant through an audit, a policy change, or a new law

## Resume conventions for this profession
- One to two pages; each role names the HR areas you owned, the size of the employee population or hiring volume, and the systems used; certifications near the top
- "Passionate about people" reads as filler; "recruited for about forty warehouse and office roles a quarter, cut average time to fill by a week" reads as credible when the figures are yours; mark any number you can't confirm and fill it in before sending

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter screen, then interviews with the HR manager and business leaders you'd support; scenario questions on employee complaints, terminations, investigations, and confidentiality are common
- Recruiter candidates may be asked to walk through their sourcing approach, write a job posting, or role-play a candidate call or offer negotiation

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $75,940 a year ($36.51 an hour); most earn between $47,180 and $128,720 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 13-1071, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 90,600 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 3 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 13-1071). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually an annual salary in corporate and government HR; coordinator and assistant roles are sometimes hourly and eligible for overtime
- Some employers add an annual bonus, and in-house recruiters may earn a per-hire bonus; agency recruiters are often paid a base plus commission on placement fees, which is a different structure worth asking about in detail

## What's usually negotiable
- Base salary within the band, title (coordinator, specialist, generalist), certification support, remote or hybrid days, and start date tend to move
- Recruiters may want to ask how any per-hire bonus or commission is calculated, and whether a placement that leaves early reduces it

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: a "one-person HR department" at a coordinator salary, recruiter roles with very high requisition loads and no coordinator support, pay described mostly as placement commission with little base, and "HR" titles that are mainly payroll data entry

## Verify before relying on this
- The US Department of Labor, the EEOC (Equal Employment Opportunity Commission), and your state labor department for employment law basics; SHRM and HRCI for certification requirements; the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook for how the field is changing

## In Canada

- **NOC 2021:** 12101; 11200, matched by title by the maintainers (not an official concordance; check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site).
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- Employment law is mostly provincial (federal rules cover banks, telecoms, and transport), and HR designations are granted by provincial bodies: HRPA in Ontario (the CHRP and CHRL designations), the CRHA through the Ordre des CRHA in Quebec, and the CPHR through CPHR Canada's member associations elsewhere; check your provincial HR association and your province's employment standards.
