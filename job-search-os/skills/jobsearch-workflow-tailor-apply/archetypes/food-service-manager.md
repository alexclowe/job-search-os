# Restaurant / Food Service Manager — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `food-service-manager` · **Aliases:** restaurant manager, shift supervisor, kitchen manager
**Also read:** `restaurant-front-of-house`, `cook`, `retail-store-manager`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Restaurant Manager, General Manager, Assistant Manager, Kitchen Manager, Shift Supervisor or Shift Leader, Food Service Manager or Director (hospitals, schools, senior living, campus dining), Banquet or Catering Manager, and Food and Beverage Manager
- Adjacent worth searching: Certified Dietary Manager roles in health care, District or Area Manager, Training Manager for a restaurant group, Bar Manager, and Operations Manager in contract food service

## Where the postings live
- Usually restaurant-group and franchise career pages, hospitality job boards, the general job boards, and contract food-service companies that staff hospitals, schools, and campuses
- Many managers are promoted from within or hired through referrals from chefs and other managers; restaurant groups often recruit from their own shift leaders first

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Food safety manager certification: many states and cities require at least one Certified Food Protection Manager, which means passing an exam from an accredited program; food handler cards for staff and an alcohol-service certificate where you serve alcohol
- The concept and volume you've run (quick service, casual, fine dining, institutional), the size of the team you've led, and your hands-on numbers work: food and labour cost, inventory, scheduling, and the point-of-sale and scheduling systems you've used
- Availability for nights, weekends, and holidays, which many postings screen on before anything else

## Story types that land
- Bringing food or labour cost back in line, and what you changed on the schedule, ordering, or prep
- A health inspection you prepared for or a food safety problem you caught and fixed
- Hiring, training, and keeping a team through a busy season or a staffing gap
- Turning around an unhappy guest or a bad online review

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Certifications near the top, then each role with the concept, rough volume or seats, team size, and results from your own records (costs, inspection scores, turnover); mark any figure you can't confirm and fill it in before sending
- "Passionate about hospitality" reads as filler; "ran the closing shift for a busy casual dining room, did weekly ordering and inventory, cut overtime by rebuilding the schedule" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a phone screen, then an interview with the general manager or area manager, and commonly a working interview or stage (a paid or unpaid shift) to see you work a real service
- Expect questions on handling a no-show staff member on a busy night, a guest complaint, a cost report that's off, and a health code issue

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $44,080 a year ($21.19 an hour); most earn between $29,940 and $65,570 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 35-1012, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 210,300 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 2 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a high school diploma or equivalent, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 35-1012). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Assistant, kitchen, and shift managers are commonly paid hourly, while general managers and food service directors are more often on an annual salary; a bonus tied to sales, food and labour cost, or inspection results is common
- Hourly managers are usually due overtime under federal and state law, while salaried managers may be classified as exempt; ask how the role is classified and what a normal week looks like, since long weeks are common in this work
- Shift differentials are uncommon, but ask about guaranteed hours, meal benefits, and how holiday shifts are paid; under federal law managers generally can't take a share of employee tip pools

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open: base salary or hourly rate, the bonus formula and how it's measured, the number of days off per week, schedule (such as a set number of closing shifts), relocation help, and paid certification
- Ask how the bonus has actually paid out in recent periods; a formula that rarely pays tends to matter more than the headline target

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: salaried "manager" roles that are mostly line or register work, "flexible availability" meaning open-ended hours, a bonus with no written formula, very high turnover in the role, and unpaid working interviews that run a full shift

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state or local health department for food safety manager and food handler rules; the ANSI National Accreditation Board's directory for accredited food protection manager programs; your state alcohol control agency for server certification; the Department of Labor for overtime and tip rules
