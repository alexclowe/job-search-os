# Product Manager — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `product-manager` · **Aliases:** product owner, technical product manager
**Also read:** `project-manager`, `ux-designer`, `marketing-manager`, `software-developer`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Associate Product Manager, Product Manager, Senior Product Manager, Group or Principal Product Manager, Product Owner, and Technical Product Manager
- Adjacent worth searching: Product Marketing Manager, Product Operations, Program Manager, Business Analyst with product ownership, and Growth Product Manager for experiment-heavy work

## Where the postings live
- Usually company career pages, the general job boards, and startup-focused boards; many product roles are also filled through referrals and internal moves from engineering, design, support, or sales
- Associate or rotational product programs tend to recruit on a campus-style calendar, so check timing if you are early in your career

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Products you owned or co-owned, the users they served, and what changed after launch, stated as outcomes rather than features
- Whether you have worked with engineers and designers day to day, and the kind of product (consumer, business software, internal tools, hardware, platform)
- Tool fluency such as Jira, spreadsheets and basic SQL often helps; product certificates (for example the Certified Scrum Product Owner from Scrum Alliance) are sometimes valued but rarely decide the screen

## Story types that land
- A time you decided what not to build, how you weighed it, and what happened
- A launch or change you drove from problem to release, and the result you measured afterwards
- A disagreement with engineering, design, or a senior stakeholder that you resolved with evidence
- Something that failed, what you learned from the data, and what you changed

## Resume conventions for this profession
- One to two pages; each role leads with the product, its users, and your scope, then two or three outcome lines
- "Spearheaded cross-functional synergy" reads as filler; "cut sign-up steps from five screens to two, and more trial users finished setup" reads as credible (use your own measured result)

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter screen, a hiring-manager call, then a loop covering product sense (design or improve a product), analytics or metrics, working with engineers, and behavioral rounds
- Some teams add a written exercise or a presentation of a past product; ask whether take-homes are paid or time-boxed

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, nearest occupation only:** there is no federal occupation code for this role, so this is Computer and Information Systems Managers, a rough guide at best: median $175,140 a year ($84.20 an hour); most earn between $107,550 and $297,510 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 11-3021, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Stock or equity:** grants aren't in these pay figures, so compare them separately.
- **Openings, nearest occupations:** about 87,500 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 2 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 11-3021). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually an annual salary; some employers add an annual bonus, and some, especially venture-backed or public tech companies, add stock or equity grants that vest over several years
- The figures above are for a nearest federal occupation, not for product managers as such; compare them with posted ranges for the exact title and level

## What's usually negotiable
- Base salary within the band, level or title, the size of any equity grant, sign-on bonus, start date, and remote or hybrid days tend to move
- Ask how the level was decided; getting placed one level higher usually moves more than negotiating inside a level

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: a product role with no engineers attached, "owns the roadmap" with no say over priorities, a product owner title that is really ticket writing, and a list of duties that belongs to three jobs

## Verify before relying on this
- The employer's own posting and level guide; published salary ranges where pay-transparency laws apply; the certification body's own site before paying for any product course
