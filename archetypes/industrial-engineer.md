# Industrial / Manufacturing Engineer — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `industrial-engineer` · **Aliases:** process engineer, quality engineer
**Also read:** `production-supervisor`, `operations-manager`, `logistics-supply-chain`, `qa-engineer`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Industrial Engineer, Manufacturing Engineer, Process Engineer, Continuous Improvement Engineer, Lean or Operational Excellence Engineer, Quality Engineer, and Senior, Lead, or Principal versions of each
- Adjacent worth searching: Operations Analyst, Continuous Improvement Manager or Specialist, Production Planning Manager, Supply Chain Analyst, Ergonomist, Plant Manager for engineers moving into leadership, and Industrial Engineering Technician for those without a degree

## Where the postings live
- Usually manufacturer, healthcare, distribution, and aerospace or defence career pages, the general job boards, and engineering recruiters; university career services often feed entry-level and co-op roles
- Many continuous-improvement roles are filled internally or through referrals from engineers and plant managers you have worked with

## What a recruiter screens for first
- An engineering degree (industrial, manufacturing, or mechanical are common), the processes and industries you know, and improvement methods such as lean, Six Sigma belts (issued by many different bodies, so name yours), time studies, and statistical process control
- Tools named in the posting, such as spreadsheets and reporting tools, computer-aided design software, simulation, the plant's enterprise resource planning system, and SQL (a database query language) for data-heavy roles
- In the US, the PE (Professional Engineer) licence is usually optional in manufacturing roles, though it can matter where you sign off public-facing work; passing the FE (Fundamentals of Engineering) exam early keeps that path open

## Story types that land
- An improvement project: the baseline, the data you gathered, the change, and the measured result in cost, time, scrap, or safety
- Launching a new line, product, or layout and getting it to rate
- Winning over operators and supervisors so a change stuck after you left the floor
- A root-cause investigation that fixed a recurring quality or downtime problem

## Resume conventions for this profession
- One page early career, two for experienced; a skills line with methods and tools, then each role with the plant or setting and one or two measured project results; degree and any licence or belt near the top for new graduates
- "Drove world-class operational excellence" reads as filler; "rebalanced an assembly line using time studies, cut the bottleneck and raised output without adding staff" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter screen, then interviews with the engineering manager and plant leaders, commonly with a project walkthrough and a floor tour; some employers give a case such as a layout or bottleneck problem
- Expect questions on the improvement methods you have used, how you handle pushback from the floor, and how you measured a result

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $102,440 a year ($49.25 an hour); most earn between $74,370 and $159,860 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 17-2112). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 23,100 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 12.4% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 17-2112). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually an annual salary; some employers add an annual bonus tied to company or plant results, and relocation help is common for plant roles
- Co-op and intern roles are often paid hourly, so ask how overtime and housing are handled

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open: base salary within the band, level or title, sign-on bonus, relocation, bonus target, paid certification or licence exam costs, and start date
- Ask how the level was decided; a higher level often moves pay more than negotiating within a level

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: an "engineer" role that is mostly supervising a shift, a long list of unrelated tools for one role, improvement targets with no support or budget, and engineer titles at technician pay

## Verify before relying on this
- NCEES, the National Council of Examiners for Engineering and Surveying (ncees.org), for the FE and PE exams and your state engineering board for licensing rules; the employer's own posting for the methods and tools; the Occupational Outlook Handbook from the BLS (Bureau of Labor Statistics) for how the field is changing

## In Canada
<!-- canada:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **NOC 2021:** 21321, matched by title by the maintainers (not an official concordance; check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site).
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- "Engineer" is a protected title in every province and territory, so using it, or approving engineering drawings and reports, calls for a licence from the provincial engineering regulator (in Alberta, APEGA); some Canadian postings say "process specialist" or "continuous improvement specialist" for this reason, so check Engineers Canada and your provincial engineering regulator before using the title.
