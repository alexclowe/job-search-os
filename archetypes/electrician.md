# Electrician — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `electrician` · **Aliases:** journeyman electrician, apprentice electrician
**Also read:** `maintenance-technician`, `hvac-technician`, `plumber`, `construction-manager`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Apprentice Electrician, Electrician Helper, Journeyman (or Journey-level) Electrician, Master Electrician, and the setting-specific versions: Residential, Commercial, Industrial, Maintenance, Service, and Controls Electrician, up to Foreman or Lead Electrician
- Adjacent worth searching: Inside Wireman, Low-Voltage or Fire Alarm Technician, Solar or Electric Vehicle Charger Installer, Electrical Estimator, Electrical Inspector, and Facilities or Plant Electrician roles inside hospitals, factories, and campuses

## Where the postings live
- Usually electrical contractor sites, the general job boards, trade-association and apprenticeship program listings, and plant or facilities career pages; union work is commonly dispatched through the local union hall's referral list rather than posted
- Many contractors hire through referrals from current crews, and apprenticeship slots often open on a set application cycle, so it tends to pay to check program dates early

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Your licence level and where it is valid: in the US, electrical licensing is usually set by the state or city, commonly moving from apprentice to journeyman to master through documented on-the-job hours, classroom training, and a licensing exam based on the electrical code the jurisdiction has adopted
- The kind of work you have done (new construction, service, industrial controls), code knowledge, reading prints, a valid driver's licence, and safety training such as an OSHA (Occupational Safety and Health Administration) 10- or 30-hour card (a course completion card, not a certification) and arc-flash or lockout/tagout training
- Whether you have your own hand tools, reliable transport to changing job sites, and the shifts or travel you can work

## Story types that land
- A job you ran or a scope you owned: the service, the panel change, the rough-in and trim-out, done to code and passed inspection
- Troubleshooting a fault others missed, and how you isolated it safely
- A safety catch: an energized circuit found, a lockout gap, a hazard you stopped work to fix
- Keeping a job on schedule with other trades, or training an apprentice

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Licence type, number, and state near the top, then certifications and safety cards, then each job with the contractor or site type, the kind of work, and the voltages or systems you worked on
- "Hard-working electrician with great attitude" reads as filler; "journeyman on commercial tenant fit-outs, conduit bending, panel terminations, fire alarm tie-ins, passed inspections first time" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a short interview with the foreman, project manager, or shop owner, commonly with code or theory questions and sometimes a written or hands-on test; apprenticeship programs usually add an aptitude test and a panel interview
- Expect questions on the work you have done, how you troubleshoot, how you handle a job that fails inspection, and your safety habits

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $30.38 an hour ($63,190 a year); most earn between $20.50 and $52.17 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 47-2111). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 72,700 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 9.2% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a high school diploma or equivalent, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 47-2111). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly: union electricians are commonly paid on the local's published union scale, with apprentices paid a rising share of the journeyman rate as they progress, plus benefit and pension contributions; non-union shops tend to pay a shop rate set by the contractor
- Overtime is common on construction and shutdown work, and federal and state rules usually set when it is owed; some contracts add shift differentials for nights, show-up pay, or pay for travel or remote jobs, and guaranteed hours are uncommon on construction, where work can stop between jobs or in bad weather

## What's usually negotiable
- At non-union shops the hourly rate, a company truck or fuel card, tool allowance, paid training toward the next licence, and steadier year-round work often move; union scale itself usually doesn't, though classification and overtime opportunity may
- Ask how hours have looked through the last slow season, and whether the shop pays for licence renewals and continuing education

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: "helper" roles with no path to a registered apprenticeship, work that sounds like licensed work offered to someone unlicensed, pay "depending on experience" with no range, no mention of safety training, and constant long-distance travel without per-diem pay

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state or city electrical licensing board (CareerOneStop's License Finder lists them; link, don't copy) for licence levels, hours, and exam rules; the Department of Labor's apprenticeship site for registered programs; the union local or contractor association for current scale

## In Canada
<!-- canada:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **NOC 2021:** 72200; 72201, matched by title by the maintainers (not an official concordance; check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site).
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- Job Bank lists construction electrician certification as compulsory in every province except British Columbia (voluntary in the territories) and industrial electrician certification as compulsory in Prince Edward Island, Quebec, and Manitoba; British Columbia has since made both trades compulsory through SkilledTradesBC, and Red Seal endorsement is available for mobility, so check your provincial apprenticeship authority for which licence your work needs.
