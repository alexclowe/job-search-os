# PT / OT Assistant — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `therapy-assistant` · **Aliases:** PTA, COTA
**Also read:** `occupational-therapist`, `nursing-assistant-caregiver`, `personal-trainer`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Physical Therapist Assistant (PTA), Licensed PTA, Certified Occupational Therapy Assistant (COTA), with the setting named: outpatient, home health, skilled nursing, acute care, pediatrics, or schools
- Adjacent worth searching: rehabilitation technician or aide, rehab coordinator, restorative nursing program roles, and per-diem or travel assistant roles

## Where the postings live
- Usually outpatient clinic chains, hospitals, skilled nursing and rehab companies, home health agencies, school districts, and the general job boards; staffing and travel agencies commonly fill contract and per-diem roles
- Many assistants are hired from their clinical rotation sites, so a strong placement often leads straight to an offer

## What a recruiter screens for first
- For PTAs: usually an accredited PTA program (accredited by the Commission on Accreditation in Physical Therapy Education), a pass on the national PTA exam from the Federation of State Boards of Physical Therapy, and a state licence or certification
- For COTAs: usually an accredited OTA program, the COTA certification exam from NBCOT, and a state licence; NBCOT certification and the state licence are separate
- The setting and patient population you know, CPR or BLS, and experience with the employer's documentation system

## Story types that land
- Progressing a patient safely through the therapist's plan and spotting when to flag a change
- Keeping documentation accurate and timely on a full schedule
- Teaching a patient or family a home program they actually followed
- Working well under a supervising therapist, including disagreeing respectfully

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Licence and certification near the top, then each role with setting, typical daily caseload, and populations (orthopedic, neuro, geriatric, pediatric)
- "Helped patients recover" reads as filler; "outpatient orthopedics, post-surgical knees and shoulders, home exercise programs" reads as credible (use your own details)

## How interviews usually run
- Often a phone screen, then an interview with the rehab director or supervising therapist; some add a clinic tour or shadow shift
- Commonly asked: how you handle a patient who refuses treatment, when you'd contact the supervising therapist, and how you keep up with productivity and notes

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $32.88 an hour ($68,380 a year); most earn between $22.96 and $43.04 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 31-2021, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 26,600 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 2 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** an associate degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 31-2021). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly, with differentials for weekends or evenings in some settings; home health often pays per visit instead, sometimes with mileage
- Overtime rules follow ordinary hourly law, but some employers track productivity (the share of paid time spent treating) instead of hours, so ask how unfinished notes after hours are paid
- Medicare pays less for some services provided in whole or part by assistants, which can shape how many assistants an employer hires and how they're scheduled; per-diem and travel roles tend to pay more per hour with fewer benefits

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open to discussion: the hourly rate, guaranteed hours (especially in skilled nursing and home health, where caseloads can drop), the productivity target, continuing-education money, and licence fees
- Ask how cancellations are handled if you're paid per visit

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: very high productivity targets, unpaid documentation time, "flexible hours" with no minimum hours promised, and assistants expected to evaluate or change plans of care, which is usually the therapist's job

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state physical therapy or occupational therapy board for licence and supervision rules; the Federation of State Boards of Physical Therapy (fsbpt.org) and NBCOT (nbcot.org) for the exams; CareerOneStop's License Finder
