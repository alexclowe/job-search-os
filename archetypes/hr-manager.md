# HR Manager — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `hr-manager` · **Aliases:** HR business partner, people operations manager
**Also read:** `hr-specialist-recruiter`, `training-specialist`, `operations-manager`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- HR (human resources) Manager, Senior HR Manager, HR Business Partner, People Operations Manager, HR Operations Manager, Employee Relations Manager, and HR Director as the next step
- Adjacent worth searching: HR Generalist (senior), Talent Acquisition Manager, Total Rewards or Compensation Manager, Labor Relations Manager, Learning and Development Manager, and People Partner at smaller companies

## Where the postings live
- Usually company career pages, the general job boards, and HR association job boards; many manager and director roles are filled through HR-focused recruiters and executive search firms
- Internal promotion from HR generalist or business partner is common, and referrals from past leaders you supported often carry weight

## What a recruiter screens for first
- The scope you've owned: headcount supported from your own record, locations or states, union or non-union workforce, and the HR areas you ran (employee relations, recruiting, benefits, compensation, compliance)
- Employment law knowledge for the states in scope, investigations experience, and the HR system named in the posting (for example Workday, UKG, ADP, or BambooHR)
- Certifications such as SHRM-CP or SHRM-SCP (from SHRM) and PHR or SPHR (from HRCI) are voluntary but often preferred for manager roles

## Story types that land
- An employee relations issue or investigation you handled fairly and documented well
- A policy, program, or system you rolled out, and how you got managers to use it
- Coaching a leader through a hard people decision, such as a performance plan or restructuring
- A retention, hiring, or engagement problem you diagnosed and improved

## Resume conventions for this profession
- A short summary with scope and certifications near the top; for each role, the population supported, locations, HR areas owned, and results from your own record such as turnover or time to fill
- "People champion" reads as filler; "handled employee relations and investigations for a multi-state workforce and rolled out a new performance process" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter screen, then interviews with the HR leader and with the business leaders you'd support; situational questions about an investigation, a termination, or a manager who ignores policy are common
- Some employers add a presentation or case, such as a plan to fix high turnover or a review of a sample policy

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $149,280 a year ($71.77 an hour); most earn between $88,200 and $267,810 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 11-3121). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 16,800 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 5.5% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 11-3121). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually an annual salary with a yearly bonus, and the bonus target tends to grow with level; HR managers are commonly classed as exempt from overtime
- Some employers pay for certification exams and recertification credits, which may be listed as a benefit rather than pay

## What's usually negotiable
- The salary within the band, bonus target, title (manager vs business partner vs director), sign-on bonus, certification and training costs, and remote days tend to move
- Ask who the role reports to and how many people it supports, because scope often decides the level and band

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: a "department of one" covering every HR area plus payroll and office management, manager titles with no authority over policy, and high turnover in the HR role itself

## Verify before relying on this
- SHRM (shrm.org) and HRCI for certification requirements; the US Department of Labor and your state labor department for employment law; the employer's posting for scope

## In Canada
<!-- canada:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **NOC 2021:** 10011, matched by title by the maintainers (not an official concordance; check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site).
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- Employment law is mostly provincial (federal rules cover banks, telecoms, and transport), and HR designations come from provincial bodies: the HRPA in Ontario, the CPHR bodies in most other provinces, and the Ordre des CRHA in Quebec, which is a professional order; check your province's employment standards and the body that grants the designation where you'll work.
