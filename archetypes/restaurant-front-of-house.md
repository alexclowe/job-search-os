# Server / Bartender / Food Counter — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `restaurant-front-of-house` · **Aliases:** server, waiter, bartender, host, barista, fast food crew
**Also read:** `cook`, `food-service-manager`, `retail-sales-associate`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Server, Waiter or Waitress, Bartender, Host or Hostess, Barista, Food Runner, Busser, Cocktail Server, Banquet Server, and Counter or Crew Member at quick-service and fast-casual spots
- Adjacent worth searching: Barback, Room Service Server, Catering or Event Server, Shift Lead or Captain, Sommelier Assistant, and Front-of-House Trainer

## Where the postings live
- Usually the general job boards, restaurant-specific job apps, the restaurant's own site with a quick-apply form, and "now hiring" signs in the window; hotels, stadiums, and caterers often post seasonal and event work
- Walking in with a short resume during a slow hour (mid-afternoon, not the rush) and asking for the manager often works faster than an online form, and many hires come through referrals from current staff

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Availability, above all nights, weekends, and holidays, and how soon you can start
- Where alcohol is served, many states or localities expect responsible alcohol service training and a minimum age to serve or pour; some places also expect a food handler card, so check your state and county
- Point-of-sale (POS) system experience, the kind of place you've worked (fine dining, casual, high-volume, bar), and how you handle a full section during a rush

## Story types that land
- Handling a full section or a slammed bar during a rush without orders going wrong
- Turning around an unhappy guest (a wrong order, a long wait) so they left satisfied
- Checking ID and cutting off service tactfully when a guest had enough
- Upselling, knowing the menu, or training new staff

## Resume conventions for this profession
- One page or less, often attached to a quick-apply form or handed over in person; list each place with its type and pace, your section or station, and the POS systems you used
- "Excellent customer service skills" reads as filler; "high-volume brunch, six-table section, trained three new servers, handled closing cash-out" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a short, informal chat with a manager on the spot or within a day or two, focused on availability, experience, and how you'd handle a difficult guest
- Many places add a paid or unpaid working interview or "stage" shift; bartender roles may include a drink-making test, and some fine-dining rooms quiz you on the menu or wine

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $15.00 an hour ($31,200 a year); most earn between $10.98 and $20.51 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 35-3023, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 1,622,900 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 6 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** no formal credential, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 35-3023). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly plus tips; in many US states an employer may pay tipped staff a lower cash wage and count part of your tips toward the minimum wage (a "tip credit"), while other states ban the tip credit and pay the full minimum wage before tips, so the base rate varies a lot by state
- Under federal rules, if your tips plus the cash wage don't reach the minimum wage in a workweek, the employer must make up the difference (DOL Fact Sheet #15), so keep your own record of hours and tips
- Under federal rules, employers, including managers and supervisors, can't keep any part of workers' tips, whether directly or through a tip pool; a manager may keep only tips a customer gives them for service they provide themselves. Tip pools that include cooks or dishwashers are allowed under federal rules only when the employer pays at least the full federal minimum wage, so ask how the pool or tip-out works and how card tips are paid out
- Mandatory service charges are often treated as the restaurant's money rather than tips, so ask how much of any service charge reaches staff
- Overtime usually applies to tipped staff after a full workweek, and some places pay a differential or higher rate for late-night, holiday, or private-event shifts
- Guaranteed hours are rare; shifts often get cut when it's slow, and some cities have scheduling laws that require advance notice or pay for last-minute changes

## What's usually negotiable
- The base rate often moves little, but which shifts and sections you get (weekend nights tend to earn the most tips), and how fast you move up, often can be discussed
- Ask how the tip pool and tip-out percentages work before accepting, since they tend to matter more than the hourly rate

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: long unpaid "training" or working-interview shifts, managers who share in the tip pool, tip-outs deducted for credit-card fees without explanation, and "flexible schedule" that may mean no set hours

## Verify before relying on this
- The Department of Labor's tipped-employee pages for tip credit and tip pool rules, and your state labor department for the tipped wage, overtime, and scheduling laws; your state or county health department for food handler rules; your state alcohol control agency for server training

## In Canada

- **NOC 2021:** 65200; 64301; 64300, matched by title by the maintainers (not an official concordance; check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site).
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- Minimum wage and tip rules are set by each province, and Quebec keeps a separate lower minimum wage for employees who receive tips; server training for alcohol is required in some provinces: in Ontario, the AGCO says anyone who sells or serves liquor must hold valid Smart Serve certification, and British Columbia generally requires a Serving It Right certificate for servers and managers, so check your province's employment standards and Job Bank's requirements.
