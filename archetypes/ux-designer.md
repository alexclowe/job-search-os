# UX / Product Designer — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `ux-designer` · **Aliases:** UX designer, UI designer, product designer, web designer
**Also read:** `graphic-designer`, `product-manager`, `software-developer`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- User Experience (UX) Designer, User Interface (UI) Designer, Product Designer, Interaction Designer, Web Designer, and the senior, lead, and principal versions of each
- Adjacent worth searching: UX Researcher, Content Designer or UX Writer, Service Designer, Design Systems Designer, UX Engineer or front-end developer (more code), and Visual Designer

## Where the postings live
- Usually company career pages, the general job boards, and design-focused boards and communities; agencies and consultancies post many roles too
- Many designers are hired through referrals and portfolio sites, and a large share of the work is freelance or contract, found through networks and past clients

## What a recruiter screens for first
- The portfolio, often before the resume: two or three case studies that show the problem, your process, and the outcome, not only final screens
- Tools named in the posting, most often Figma, plus prototyping, design systems, and some comfort with HTML and CSS
- The kind of product (consumer app, business software, e-commerce, government service) and whether you have worked alongside engineers and product managers; accessibility knowledge is increasingly valued

## Story types that land
- A design that changed user behaviour, and how you measured it (task success, sign-ups, fewer support tickets)
- Research you ran or used that changed the direction of a design
- A disagreement with product or engineering about a design, and how you reached a decision
- A constraint (time, legacy system, accessibility rules) that shaped a better solution

## Resume conventions for this profession
- One page is common; the portfolio link sits at the top, and each role names the product, your part in it, and a result; tools listed briefly
- "Created delightful, pixel-perfect experiences" reads as filler; "redesigned the checkout form so fewer people abandoned it at the address step" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter screen, a portfolio presentation to a panel, then interviews with product and engineering partners and a design critique
- Many teams add a design exercise, live (a whiteboard or Figma challenge) or take-home; ask whether a take-home is paid or time-boxed

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $104,000 a year ($50.00 an hour); most earn between $53,750 and $201,550 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 15-1255). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 8,300 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 6.0% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 15-1255). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
- **Self-employed:** about 13% of people in this work are self-employed, and the pay figures above leave them out (BLS Projections 2025–35).
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually an annual salary; some employers add a yearly bonus
- Freelance and contract designers commonly charge hourly or by project and cover their own benefits and taxes, so compare total pay rather than the rate alone

## What's usually negotiable
- Base salary within the band, level or title, remote or hybrid days, start date, and budget for tools, courses, or conferences tend to move
- Ask how the level was decided; a portfolio that shows senior-level scope often moves the level more than negotiating inside it

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: one designer expected to do brand, marketing, UX, UI, and front-end code; unpaid "spec work" disguised as an exercise; "UX" titles that are mostly graphic production; and no mention of research or users at all

## Verify before relying on this
- The employer's posting and design team pages; published salary ranges where pay-transparency laws apply; the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook for how web and digital design work is changing

- Design titles aren't regulated in Canada, so the portfolio usually matters more than any credential; a user experience engineer title raises the "engineer" issue, since that title is protected in every province and territory and Alberta exempts only "software engineer" (per Engineers Canada and APEGA), so check your provincial engineering regulator, and check the language requirement on roles serving Quebec.
