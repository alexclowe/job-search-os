# Insurance Sales Agent — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `insurance-sales-agent` · **Aliases:** insurance agent, insurance producer, broker (insurance), account manager (insurance), sales agent (insurance)
**Also read:** `financial-advisor`, `claims-adjuster`, `customer-service-rep`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Insurance Agent, Insurance Sales Agent, Insurance Producer, Insurance Broker, with the line named: property and casualty, personal lines, commercial lines, life and health, or benefits
- Adjacent worth searching: Account Manager or Client Service Representative at an agency (often salaried, service-focused, and often still licensed), Benefits Consultant, and Underwriting Sales Representative

## Where the postings live
- Usually agency and carrier career pages, the general job boards, and state or regional insurance association boards; captive carriers often recruit continuously for new agents
- Many agency roles are filled through referrals from producers and customer-service staff already at the agency

## What a recruiter screens for first
- The state producer licence for the lines the role sells, or willingness to get it: usually a state pre-licensing course and state exam for each line (property and casualty, life and health), then an appointment with each carrier you sell for; other state licences may be needed for multi-state books
- Sales history in plain numbers from your own record, whether you'd bring an existing book of business, and comfort with the agency's quoting and management systems

## Story types that land
- Matching coverage to a client's real risks, including explaining what a policy doesn't cover
- Building or retaining a book of business: renewals kept, cross-sells made, referrals earned
- Winning back or keeping a client after a claim or a rate increase
- Keeping records and renewals clean enough that nothing lapsed

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Licences with lines and states near the top; for each role, the lines sold, the client type (personal or commercial), and results from your own record, such as policies written or retention
- "Top performer" and "exceeded quota" read as filler without a number from your own record behind them; mark any figure you can't confirm and fill it in before sending

## How interviews usually run
- Often a phone screen, then an interview with the agency owner or sales manager; role-plays such as "sell me this policy" or handling a price objection are common
- Captive carrier roles may include a group information session, an aptitude or personality assessment, and questions about how you'd find your first clients

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $62,280 a year ($29.94 an hour); most earn between $37,330 and $138,140 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 41-3021). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 43,100 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 3.3% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a high school diploma or equivalent, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 41-3021). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
- **Self-employed:** about 12% of people in this work are self-employed, and the pay figures above leave them out (BLS Projections 2025–35).
<!-- facts:end -->
- Commonly commission on new policies plus a smaller renewal commission, sometimes with a base salary or a draw against commission during the first months; service-focused account-manager roles are more often salaried with a bonus
- Captive agents sell one company's products; independent agents place business with several carriers. Pay, who owns the book of business, and lead support usually differ between the two
- Captive agents who own an agency may pay their own office and staff costs, so income after expenses can differ a lot from gross commission
- Advanced commissions are commonly charged back if a policy lapses or is cancelled early, so ask how chargebacks work and whether you would owe money after leaving; ask too whether any draw is recoverable (paid back from later commission) or non-recoverable

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open to discussion: the base or draw and how long it lasts, the new and renewal commission rates, who owns the book if you leave, lead sources and marketing support, and licensing costs
- Ask for the draw clawback terms and the non-compete or non-solicit language in writing before accepting

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: commission-only with no leads or training, fees to start or to buy leads, "unlimited income" with no base or typical first-year figures, pressure to sell first to friends and family, and recruiting described more than selling

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state department of insurance and the National Insurance Producer Registry (NIPR) for licence requirements by line; the NAIC for consumer and licensing references; the agency agreement for commission, draw, and book-ownership terms

- Insurance agents and brokers are licensed provincially, and life insurance licensing commonly runs through the Life Licence Qualification Program (LLQP); general insurance brokers are regulated separately in some provinces (in Ontario, RIBO licenses brokers and FSRA licenses agents), so check your provincial insurance regulator.
