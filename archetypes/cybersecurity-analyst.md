# Cybersecurity Analyst — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `cybersecurity-analyst` · **Aliases:** information security analyst, SOC analyst, penetration tester
**Also read:** `sysadmin-cloud`, `it-support`, `compliance-officer`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Cybersecurity Analyst, Information Security Analyst, Security Operations Center (SOC) Analyst, Security Engineer, and the senior and lead versions of each
- Adjacent worth searching: Incident Responder, Threat Intelligence Analyst, Vulnerability Management Analyst, Penetration Tester, Governance, Risk and Compliance (GRC) Analyst, Identity and Access Management Analyst, and Information Systems Security Officer

## Where the postings live
- Usually company career pages, the general job boards, and government job sites; managed security service providers (companies that watch other firms' networks) post many entry roles
- Many roles tied to government or defence contracts are posted by contractors and ask for a security clearance, and referrals from local security meetups and capture-the-flag events often help

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Hands-on work with the tools in the posting: a security information and event management (SIEM) tool such as Splunk or Microsoft Sentinel, endpoint detection tools, firewalls, vulnerability scanners, and scripting in Python or PowerShell
- Certifications are often valued, sometimes as a filter for government-linked roles: CompTIA Security+ at entry level; ISC2's Certified Information Systems Security Professional (CISSP) for experienced staff (it asks for several years of full-time security work, and people who pass the exam without them can become an Associate of ISC2 while they build it); ISACA's Certified Information Security Manager (CISM) for security managers; and hands-on penetration testing certifications for offensive roles
- Clearance eligibility and willingness to work shifts in a monitoring center

## Story types that land
- An alert you investigated: what tipped you off, how you ruled things in or out, and what you did
- A weakness you found and got fixed, including how you convinced the owner to prioritize it
- A process you improved: fewer false alarms, faster patching, a clearer playbook
- Explaining a security risk to someone non-technical so they made a better decision

## Resume conventions for this profession
- One to two pages; tools and certifications near the top with the issuer; home-lab work, capture-the-flag results, and write-ups count when you are new
- "Protected the organization from all threats" reads as inflated; "tuned the login-alert rules so the team stopped chasing false alarms and caught a real password-spraying attempt" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter screen, then a technical interview with scenario questions ("you see many failed logins from one address; what next?"), networking and operating-system basics, and how attacks usually work
- Some employers add a practical: review logs, triage a sample alert, or write up an incident; offensive roles may include a hands-on lab

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $129,180 a year ($62.11 an hour); most earn between $75,090 and $199,850 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 15-1212). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 14,100 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 21.0% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 15-1212). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually an annual salary; some employers add a yearly bonus or a certification bonus, and monitoring-center roles may pay extra for night or weekend shifts
- Ask how on-call for incidents is handled and paid; contract roles, common in government work, tend to pay hourly with fewer benefits

## What's usually negotiable
- Base salary within the band, title or level, shift choice, paid certification exams and training, remote or hybrid days, and start date tend to move
- Ask whether a clearance the employer sponsors comes with a pay step once granted; an active clearance often adds bargaining weight

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: an "entry-level" posting asking for senior certifications, one analyst covering monitoring, engineering, and compliance alone, unpaid on-call for incidents, and vague "other duties" in a monitoring-center role with no shift details

## Verify before relying on this
- The certification body's own site (CompTIA, ISC2, ISACA) for current experience rules; the NICE Workforce Framework from NIST (the US National Institute of Standards and Technology) for how roles are defined; the employer's posting for clearance terms

## In Canada

- **NOC 2021:** 21220, matched by title by the maintainers (not an official concordance; check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site).
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- Cybersecurity isn't a regulated profession in Canada, but many federal and defence-related roles ask for a government security screening level, which can take time and may depend on residency history; check the screening level on the posting before applying.
- "Engineer" is a protected title in every province and territory (Alberta exempts only the title "software engineer"; per Engineers Canada and APEGA), so using a security engineer title without a provincial engineering licence can breach the law, and Canadian postings often use another title for this role (per Engineers Canada); check your provincial engineering regulator before using it.
