# Production / Assembly Worker — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `manufacturing-worker` · **Aliases:** assembler, quality inspector, machine operator
**Also read:** `production-supervisor`, `maintenance-technician`, `welder`, `warehouse-associate`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Production Worker, Production Associate, Assembler, Assembly Line Worker, Machine Operator, Manufacturing Associate, Packaging Operator, and Quality Inspector or Quality Checker
- Adjacent worth searching: Line Lead or Team Lead, Material Handler, CNC or Machine Setup Operator (a step up), Quality Technician, and Maintenance Helper

## Where the postings live
- Usually the general job boards, manufacturer career sites with quick-apply forms, and staffing agencies that fill plant jobs on a temp-to-hire basis; local workforce centers often list openings and host hiring events
- Many plants hire through agencies first and convert people to direct hire later, and referrals from current employees commonly carry weight

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Whether you can work the shift (plants often run nights, weekends, or rotating schedules), reliable transport, and whether you can handle the physical work in the posting
- Basic math and measuring (reading a tape measure, calipers, or gauges), following written work instructions or blueprints, and any experience with the plant's type of product or machine
- A clean safety record; many plants run a background check and drug screen, and some give a short skills or mechanical aptitude test

## Story types that land
- Hitting your line's output while keeping scrap or defects low
- Catching a defect or an out-of-spec part and stopping it before it shipped
- Learning several stations and filling in wherever the line needed you
- Suggesting a small fix to a process that saved time or improved safety

## Resume conventions for this profession
- One page, often replaced by the online form; for each job, name the product type, the machines or tools you ran, and any quality or safety training
- "Team player" reads as filler; "ran two injection-molding machines on nights, did hourly quality checks, trained on three assembly stations" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a short phone screen or agency interview, then a meeting with a supervisor, a plant tour, and questions about attendance, shift flexibility, and safety
- Some plants add a basic math, reading, or mechanical test, a hands-on dexterity check, a drug screen, and a background check before an offer

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $21.47 an hour ($44,650 a year); most earn between $16.00 and $31.19 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 51-2090, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 272,700 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 4 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a high school diploma or equivalent, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 51-2090). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly, with shift differentials for second and third shifts or weekend crews at many plants; some add attendance, safety, or production bonuses
- Production workers are usually non-exempt, so overtime pay applies after a full workweek under federal law, and overtime can be frequent or mandatory in busy periods
- Guaranteed hours are uncommon; ask how the plant handles slowdowns (shortened weeks, temporary layoffs) and whether any minimum hours apply
- Unionized plants usually publish wage steps and seniority rules in the contract, and agency temps often earn a different rate until they convert

## What's usually negotiable
- The entry rate often moves little, but shift choice, differential eligibility, a higher rate for skills like machine setup, and the timing of conversion from agency to direct hire may be open
- Where a union contract applies, placement on the wage scale and seniority date usually matter more than the rate itself

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: "mandatory overtime as needed" with no limit, temp roles that seem to stay temp indefinitely, vague safety language in a plant with heavy machinery, and constant reposting of the same job

## Verify before relying on this
- OSHA's pages for machine guarding and plant safety rules; the Department of Labor's overtime pages and your state labor department; the posting, offer letter, or union contract for shift, rate, and differentials
