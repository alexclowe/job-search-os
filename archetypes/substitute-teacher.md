# Substitute Teacher — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `substitute-teacher` · **Aliases:** guest teacher
**Also read:** `teacher`, `teaching-assistant`, `tutor`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Substitute Teacher, Guest Teacher, Day-to-Day Substitute, Building Substitute (assigned to one school), Long-Term Substitute, and substitutes by level or subject (elementary, secondary, special education, ESL, preschool)
- Adjacent worth searching: paraprofessional or teaching assistant, tutor, after-school program staff, and full-time teacher roles if you hold or are working toward a teaching licence

## Where the postings live
- Usually district career pages and the district's substitute-management system, plus staffing agencies that many districts use to hire and place substitutes
- Many districts recruit substitutes year-round, with a push before each term; a building-sub or long-term opening is often filled from the substitutes a principal already knows

## What a recruiter screens for first
- The state's substitute permit or certificate, which varies widely: some states ask for a teaching licence, others a bachelor's degree or some college credits, plus a fingerprint background check and sometimes a short training course
- Classroom or youth experience, the grades and subjects you'll cover, and how many days a week you're available on short notice

## Story types that land
- Walking into an unfamiliar class and keeping it on track with the absent teacher's plan
- Handling a behaviour problem calmly and leaving a clear note for the teacher
- Covering a long-term assignment: planning, grading, and parent contact
- Being asked back by a school or teacher because of how you ran the day

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Permit or licence near the top, then the schools or districts served, grade bands and subjects covered, and any long-term assignments with their length described in words
- "Great with kids" reads as filler; "regular substitute at a middle school, grades six to eight, including a long-term science assignment" reads as credible (use your own details)

## How interviews usually run
- Often an online application and orientation, then a short interview or none at all for day-to-day roles; long-term assignments usually add an interview with the principal
- Commonly asked: how you'd handle a class that won't settle, what you do when there's no lesson plan, and how you follow safety and reporting rules

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $20.03 an hour ($41,670 a year); most earn between $13.73 and $31.63 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 25-3031). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 65,000 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 2.0% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 25-3031). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually a daily rate, with a half-day rate for shorter assignments; long-term substitutes often earn a higher day rate, and some districts move them onto the teacher salary schedule after a stretch in the same role
- Day-to-day substitutes often get few or no benefits; agency substitutes are paid by the agency, whose rate may differ from the district's own

## What's usually negotiable
- The published day rate rarely moves; what tends to move is which schools and grades you accept, building-sub or long-term assignments, and whether a long-term role pays on the teacher schedule
- Ask whether the district pays more for hard-to-fill days, special-education classrooms, or a certain number of days worked

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: long-term roles paid at the day-to-day rate, substitutes routinely covering several classes at once, agency roles that charge fees, and expectations to plan and grade without extra pay

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state department of education for substitute permit rules; the district or agency HR page for the day rate and application steps; any union contract that covers long-term substitutes
