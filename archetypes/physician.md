# Physician — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `physician` · **Aliases:** doctor, hospitalist, family medicine, surgeon
**Also read:** `nurse-practitioner`, `healthcare-administrator`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Physician or Medical Doctor with the specialty and setting named: Family Medicine, Internal Medicine, Hospitalist, Pediatrician, Psychiatrist, Anesthesiologist, Emergency Medicine, Surgeon, and so on; plus Attending, Associate Medical Director, and Medical Director
- Adjacent worth searching: locum tenens (temporary) roles, telehealth physician, occupational medicine, physician advisor or utilization review, and industry roles such as medical science liaison or medical monitor

## Where the postings live
- Usually health-system and group-practice career pages, specialty society job boards, and physician recruiters (in-house and agency); many roles are filled through residency and fellowship networks before they're posted
- Locum and telehealth roles commonly run through staffing agencies, and academic roles through university job sites

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Licensing path in plain words: a medical degree (MD or DO), the national licensing exams (the USMLE for MD graduates; DO graduates take COMLEX-USA and can also take the USMLE, which some residency programs look for), a residency, then a licence from each state medical board where you'll practise; board certification in your specialty is commonly expected or expected within a set time
- A federal DEA registration for prescribing controlled substances, hospital privileges or eligibility for them, and a clean history on the credentialing questions
- The specialty fit, procedures you perform, patient volume you're used to, call expectations, and visa status for international graduates

## Story types that land
- A diagnosis you caught or a complication you managed well, told without identifying details
- Improving a quality, safety, or access measure in your clinic or unit
- Leading a care team, teaching residents, or resolving a conflict with a colleague
- Handling a hard conversation with a patient or family

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Usually a CV rather than a resume: education, residency and fellowship, board certification, licences with states, then work history, publications, teaching and committees; length grows with experience
- Plain descriptions of practice scope (setting, patient mix, procedures) read as credible; broad claims about "excellence" usually add nothing

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter call, then a site visit that can run a full day: meetings with physicians, the department chief, administrators and sometimes nurses, plus a dinner with future colleagues and sometimes a spouse or partner
- Commonly discussed: call schedule, patient volume, support staff, how productivity is measured, and partnership or academic-promotion tracks

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $265,930 a year ($127.85 an hour); most earn between $69,170 and $452,360 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 29-1229, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 22,100 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 17 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a doctoral or professional degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 29-1229). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Commonly an annual salary, a salary plus a productivity or quality bonus, or pay based on productivity measured in work RVUs (relative value units, a standard weight for each service); many new contracts pay a guaranteed salary for an initial period and then move to productivity
- Hospitalists and emergency physicians are often paid per shift, and locum work usually pays a daily or hourly rate through an agency; academic roles tend to pay a lower salary with other benefits
- Other common pieces: sign-on and relocation bonuses, student-loan repayment, call pay, continuing medical education money, and malpractice coverage

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open to discussion: base salary and how long the salary guarantee lasts, the dollar amount paid per work RVU and the bonus thresholds, sign-on and relocation, loan repayment, call frequency and pay, continuing-education time, and start date
- Ask whether malpractice is claims-made or occurrence and who pays for "tail" coverage if you leave; ask too about the non-compete radius and length, and whether any recruitment money is a loan that's forgiven only if you stay

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: no tail coverage, a wide non-compete, productivity targets set with no support staff, bonus formulas the employer won't show in writing, and call schedules that are "shared" without numbers

## Verify before relying on this
- The state medical board and the Federation of State Medical Boards (fsmb.org) for licensure requirements; the USMLE or the osteopathic exam board for exam rules; your specialty board for certification; a health-care attorney to review the contract

## In Canada
<!-- canada:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **NOC 2021:** 31100; 31101; 31102, matched by title by the maintainers (not an official concordance; check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site).
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- Job Bank lists national qualifying examinations and a licence from the provincial or territorial licensing authority, and many physicians bill the provincial health plan fee-for-service rather than drawing a salary; internationally trained physicians often face extra assessment steps, including credential checks and exams through the Medical Council of Canada, so check your provincial licensing authority first (in Quebec, the Collège des médecins du Québec).
