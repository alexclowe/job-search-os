# Delivery / Light Truck Driver — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `delivery-driver` · **Aliases:** route driver, courier, driver/sales worker
**Also read:** `truck-driver`, `warehouse-associate`, `inventory-shipping-clerk`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Delivery Driver, Route Driver, Package Delivery Driver, Van Driver, Courier, Box Truck Driver, and Driver/Sales Worker or Route Sales Representative (for bread, beverage, or snack routes)
- Adjacent worth searching: Parts Delivery Driver, Pharmacy or Medical Courier, Furniture or Appliance Delivery Driver, Dispatcher, and CDL training programs if you want to move up to heavy trucks

## Where the postings live
- Usually the general job boards, company career sites with quick-apply forms, delivery service partners that contract with large carriers, and staffing agencies; local businesses (parts stores, pharmacies, florists) often post small ads or hire by referral
- App-based delivery platforms sign people up as independent contractors through the app rather than through a job posting, which tends to be a very different arrangement from an employee driving job

## What a recruiter screens for first
- A valid driver's licence and a clean driving record (the employer usually pulls your motor vehicle record), plus age and experience rules set by the employer or its insurer
- For heavier vans and box trucks driven across state lines, a DOT (Department of Transportation) medical card may be needed; a commercial driver's licence (CDL) usually isn't needed for light trucks
- Whether you can lift and carry packages all day, work in all weather, and read a route or handheld scanner; route sales roles also look for customer and selling experience

## Story types that land
- Finishing a heavy route on time, and how you planned around traffic, weather, or a late truck
- A clean safety record and good habits (vehicle inspections, backing carefully, securing loads)
- Handling a damaged, missing, or refused delivery and keeping the customer informed
- For route sales, growing orders or fixing a problem with a regular customer

## Resume conventions for this profession
- One page, often replaced by an online form; list your licence type and state, then each job with the vehicle type, the route type (residential, business, multi-stop), and your safety record
- "Safe driver" reads as filler; "multi-stop residential route in a cargo van, handheld scanner, no preventable accidents" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a quick phone screen, then an in-person interview, a motor vehicle record check, a drug screen, and sometimes a road test or ride-along
- Many employers hire fast and train on the job, and questions usually focus on your driving record, availability, and how you'd handle a late or damaged delivery

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $21.57 an hour ($44,860 a year); most earn between $14.81 and $38.61 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 53-3033, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 152,700 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 2 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a high school diploma or equivalent, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 53-3033). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly for employee drivers; some jobs pay per route or per day, and route sales drivers may earn a base plus a share of sales
- Overtime matters here: many drivers of vehicles in the lighter weight class are covered by federal overtime even when the company is a motor carrier (the small vehicle exception), while drivers of heavier trucks on interstate work may be exempt; per-route or day-rate pay usually still earns overtime for non-exempt drivers, so ask how long weeks are paid
- If you drive your own car, ask how mileage or vehicle costs are reimbursed, since that can change take-home pay a lot
- Some employers pay a differential for early-morning, weekend, or peak-season routes, and guaranteed hours or a guaranteed daily minimum are uncommon but worth asking about
- App-based contractors are usually paid per delivery and pay their own fuel, insurance, and car costs, so compare net income after expenses rather than the gross payout

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open: your route or schedule, peak-season extra pay, and for route sales roles the base and the share of sales; the hourly rate at entry level tends to move little
- Ask for any per-route or per-day pay terms in writing, including what happens when a route runs long

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: "earn up to" figures with no typical pay, per-route pay with routes that commonly run long, contractor status for a job that looks like an employee job, and costs for vehicle damage deducted from pay

## Verify before relying on this
- The Department of Labor's motor carrier exemption fact sheet for overtime; FMCSA's pages for medical card rules; your state driver licensing agency and state labor department; the written pay plan before accepting

- A regular provincial driver's licence usually covers light delivery vehicles, while heavier trucks need a higher licence class; overtime and employee-versus-contractor rules usually come from provincial employment standards, but couriers and carriers that cross provincial borders fall under the Canada Labour Code instead, so check your provincial driver licensing office and your province's employment standards.
