# Public Relations / Communications Specialist — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `pr-specialist` · **Aliases:** communications specialist, PR coordinator
**Also read:** `writer-editor`, `marketing-specialist`, `social-media-manager`, `marketing-manager`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Public Relations (PR) Specialist, PR Coordinator, Communications Specialist, Corporate Communications Specialist, Media Relations Specialist, Public Affairs Specialist, Public Information Officer, and Communications Manager as the next step
- Adjacent worth searching: Internal Communications Specialist, Community Relations Coordinator, Content Specialist, Account Executive or Account Coordinator at a PR agency, Investor Relations Associate, and Speechwriter

## Where the postings live
- Usually PR and communications agencies, company and nonprofit career pages, universities, hospitals, and government job sites (public information and public affairs roles), plus the general job boards and communications association boards
- Agency roles often hire in cohorts at the coordinator level, and many mid-level roles are filled through referrals from journalists, agency alumni, and professional networks

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Writing samples: press releases, pitches, media coverage you secured, internal announcements, speeches, or social posts, with your role in each made clear
- Media relations experience, crisis or issues work, the audiences and sectors you know, and tools named in the posting such as media databases, monitoring tools, WordPress, or Canva
- A degree in communications, journalism, or public relations is commonly listed; an accreditation in public relations is usually optional and more often seen at mid-career

## Story types that land
- A story you pitched and placed: the angle, who you pitched, and what coverage followed
- A crisis or sensitive announcement you helped handle: what was said, when, and by whom
- An internal campaign that changed what employees knew or did
- Preparing a spokesperson or leader for an interview or public event

## Resume conventions for this profession
- A one-page resume with a link to a portfolio or coverage list; for each role, the sectors, audiences, and channels, and results from your own record such as placements or campaign reach
- "Generated massive buzz" reads as filler; "pitched and placed a feature on the product launch in trade publications" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter screen, then an interview with the communications lead, then a round with executives or clients; crisis scenarios such as "a reporter calls about a leaked memo" are common
- Many employers add a writing test, such as drafting a press release, a pitch, or a holding statement under time pressure

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $74,750 a year ($35.94 an hour); most earn between $44,110 and $135,150 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 27-3031). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 23,000 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 1.8% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 27-3031). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually an annual salary; agency roles may add a bonus tied to client retention or new business, and government roles tend to follow a published pay scale
- Roles that include after-hours media or crisis duty may or may not pay extra for it, so ask how on-call time is handled

## What's usually negotiable
- The salary within the band, title (coordinator, specialist, manager), remote days, professional development or accreditation costs, and at agencies the billable-hours target tend to move
- Ask how after-hours media duty is shared and whether it comes with extra pay or time off

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: one person expected to cover PR, social media, design, events, and marketing, "must be available at all times" language, coverage targets presented as guarantees, and a coordinator title with a manager's workload

## Verify before relying on this
- Public relations associations for accreditation requirements and ethics codes; the employer's posting for the channels and audiences; government job sites for published pay scales on public-sector roles
