# Nursing Assistant / Home Health Aide — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `nursing-assistant-caregiver` · **Aliases:** CNA, patient care tech, home health aide, personal care aide, caregiver
**Also read:** `nurse`, `licensed-practical-nurse`, `medical-assistant`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Certified Nursing Assistant (CNA), Nurse Aide, Patient Care Assistant or Patient Care Technician, Home Health Aide, Personal Care Aide, Caregiver, and in some states State Tested Nursing Assistant (STNA) or Licensed Nursing Assistant (LNA)
- Adjacent worth searching: Certified Medication Aide where the state offers it, Restorative Aide, Hospice Aide, Unit Secretary or Monitor Tech in hospitals, and Behavioral Health Technician

## Where the postings live
- Usually nursing homes, assisted living and memory care communities, hospitals, home health and hospice agencies, and home care franchises; the general job boards and facility career pages carry most of the volume
- Many long-term care facilities hire year-round and some offer paid training toward certification, so calling the staffing coordinator or walking in can be faster than the online form
- Private-pay caregiving is often found through home care agencies, caregiver marketplaces, or word of mouth rather than a formal posting

## What a recruiter screens for first
- For a CNA role: completion of a state-approved nurse aide training program, a passed state competency exam, and a listing in good standing on the state nurse aide registry; nursing homes must check the registry before you start, and home health agencies often do
- Home health and personal care aide roles vary more by state and by agency; many ask for CPR, a clean background check, a driver's licence and a reliable car
- Knockout questions tend to come first: which shifts and weekends you can work, whether you can lift and transfer patients, and whether you can start soon

## Story types that land
- Noticing that a resident or client seemed different (eating less, more confused, a new sore) and telling the nurse in time
- Helping someone who resisted care (bathing, eating, moving) while keeping their dignity
- Keeping up on a heavy assignment without cutting corners on safety, such as fall prevention or safe transfers
- Being the steady person a family trusted during a hard stretch

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Certification and registry state near the top, then CPR or BLS (Basic Life Support) with expiry dates; each job names the setting (nursing home, hospital unit, home care) and the kind of residents or clients
- Short and plain works best: one page is usually enough; list the daily care you gave (bathing, feeding, transfers with a lift, vital signs, intake and output charting)
- "Passionate caregiver" reads as filler; "memory care unit, assisted residents with meals, transfers and toileting, charted intake and output each shift" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a short phone screen, then an in-person interview with the staffing coordinator or a nurse; some facilities hire on the spot the same day
- Common questions tend to ask what you would do if a resident fell, refused care, or seemed suddenly unwell, and how you handle a hard family member; many ask directly about weekend and holiday availability
- Home care agencies may add a skills check, a background and reference check, and a match meeting with the client or family

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $17.21 an hour ($35,800 a year); most earn between $13.00 and $21.65 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 31-1120, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 956,700 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 2 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a high school diploma or equivalent, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 31-1120). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly, with shift differentials for evenings, nights, and weekends at many facilities; overtime and picked-up shifts often make a big difference to take-home pay
- Ask about guaranteed hours: home care work can depend on client schedules, and hours may drop when a client goes to hospital or passes away
- Agency and per-diem shifts tend to pay a higher hourly rate with fewer benefits; home care aides may or may not be paid for travel time between clients, so ask
- Unionized nursing homes and hospitals commonly publish wage steps in the contract

## What's usually negotiable
- The starting rate within the facility's range (often tied to certification and years of experience), shift and weekend differentials, and sign-on bonus terms tend to move
- Paid training, tuition help toward an LPN or RN, a set schedule, and mileage pay for home care are often worth asking about

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: "flexible hours" with no minimum, a sign-on bonus you would pay back if you leave early, mandatory overtime language, a resident count the posting won't state, and home care roles that don't mention mileage or travel time
- Paid training that ties you to a long work commitment may be fine, but ask what you would owe if you left

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state nurse aide registry and the state health department for training, exam, and renewal rules; the federal nurse aide training and registry rules in the Code of Federal Regulations (42 CFR part 483) on ecfr.gov for nursing homes; CareerOneStop's License Finder for home care aide rules in your state
