# Graphic Designer — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `graphic-designer` · **Aliases:** visual designer, art director
**Also read:** `ux-designer`, `photographer`, `marketing-specialist`, `interior-designer`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Graphic Designer, Junior or Senior Graphic Designer, Visual Designer, Brand Designer, Production Artist, Publications Designer, Art Director, and Senior or Associate Creative Director
- Adjacent worth searching: Marketing Designer, Digital or Web Designer, Motion Designer, Packaging Designer, Presentation Designer, Production Designer at a printer, and UI or product design roles for people moving toward screens

## Where the postings live
- Usually in-house marketing teams, advertising and design agencies, publishers, and printers, posted on company career pages, the general job boards, and design-specific boards and portfolio communities
- Many roles, and most freelance work, come through referrals, creative staffing agencies, and people who have seen your portfolio, so an up-to-date online portfolio often does more than any single application

## What a recruiter screens for first
- The portfolio: a small set of strong, relevant pieces, each with a line on the brief, your role, and the outcome; many screeners look at it before the resume
- The tools named in the posting (for example Adobe Illustrator, InDesign, Photoshop, After Effects, Figma, or Canva) and the kind of work asked for: brand, print, digital, social, packaging, or motion
- A design degree or diploma is commonly listed, but a strong portfolio often counts for more

## Story types that land
- A brief you took from rough request to finished design: the problem, the options you showed, and why the final one worked
- Handling feedback from several stakeholders without losing the idea
- Building or applying a brand system so a team could produce on-brand work quickly
- A tight deadline or print or production problem you solved

## Resume conventions for this profession
- A one-page resume that points to the portfolio link near the top, with a tools line; many designers keep the resume itself clean and simple so it reads well in applicant tracking systems
- "Creative visionary" reads as filler; "designed the packaging refresh for a product line and prepared print-ready files" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter screen, then a portfolio walk-through with the design lead where you explain your choices, then a round with marketing or creative partners
- Some employers set a design exercise; a short or paid exercise is common, while a long unpaid one that looks like real client work is worth questioning

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $62,960 a year ($30.27 an hour); most earn between $39,520 and $104,910 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 27-1024, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 28,200 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 2 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 27-1024). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
- **Self-employed:** about 20% of people in this work are self-employed, and the pay figures above leave them out (BLS Projections 2025–35).
<!-- facts:end -->
- In-house and agency roles usually pay an annual salary, sometimes with a small bonus; contract roles through creative staffing agencies tend to pay hourly
- Freelance designers are commonly paid per project, by the hour, or on a monthly retainer; a freelance rate has to cover self-employment tax, health insurance, software, equipment, and unbilled time spent finding work and doing admin, so it isn't comparable to a salaried rate at face value
- Many designers mix a salaried role with side freelance work, so check the employment contract for any rules about outside work or who owns designs you create

## What's usually negotiable
- For salaried roles, the salary, title (designer vs senior vs art director), remote days, and a software or training allowance tend to move
- For freelance work, the scope, number of revision rounds, deposit and payment terms, and who owns the final files and rights are often worth settling in writing before starting

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: one role asking for graphic design, video, web development, copywriting, and social media management at once, "exposure" instead of pay, unpaid spec work, and unlimited revisions in freelance terms

## Verify before relying on this
- Industry associations' published salary and freelance rate surveys; the employer's posting and the contract's intellectual property clause; your tax adviser or the IRS small business pages for self-employment tax if you freelance

- "Graphic designer" isn't a regulated title in Canada, though the Association of Registered Graphic Designers, created by Ontario legislation, holds the RGD designation, which some employers recognize; freelancers must register for GST/HST with the CRA once their taxable sales pass the small-supplier threshold (and may register voluntarily before that), so check the CRA's guidance and whether a role serving Quebec asks for French.
