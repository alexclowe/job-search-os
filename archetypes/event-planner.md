# Meeting & Event Planner — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `event-planner` · **Aliases:** event coordinator, conference planner
**Also read:** `executive-assistant`, `marketing-specialist`, `food-service-manager`, `project-manager`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Event Planner, Event Coordinator, Meeting Planner, Conference Planner or Manager, Special Events Coordinator, Events Manager, Convention Services Manager (on the venue side), Catering or Banquet Sales Manager, and Wedding Planner
- Adjacent worth searching: Trade Show or Exhibit Coordinator, Incentive Travel Planner, Hospitality Sales Manager, Destination Management Company roles, Donor or Fundraising Events Manager at nonprofits, Marketing Events Manager, and Executive Assistant roles with heavy event duties

## Where the postings live
- Usually company, hotel, venue, university, association, and nonprofit career pages, hospitality and events job boards, and the general boards; planning agencies and destination management companies post both staff and freelance roles
- Many roles, and most freelance work, come through referrals from venues, caterers, and past clients; industry association chapters and their job boards are commonly where planners find each other

## What a recruiter screens for first
- The type and size of events you've run (corporate meetings, conferences, weddings, fundraisers, trade shows), budgets you've managed from your own records, and whether you've handled contracts with hotels and vendors
- Registration and event software, spreadsheet and budget skills, and credentials that often help, such as the Certified Meeting Professional (CMP) from the Events Industry Council or the Certified Special Events Professional (CSEP); a hospitality or marketing degree is common, though many employers weigh experience more
- Travel availability and willingness to work nights and weekends around events

## Story types that land
- An event where something went wrong on the day (a speaker no-show, weather, a vendor failure) and how you recovered
- Negotiating a hotel or venue contract, including the terms you protected (attendance commitments, cancellation, food and beverage minimums)
- Bringing an event in on budget, or growing attendance or sponsorship from your own records
- Managing many stakeholders: a client, a boss, volunteers, and vendors pulling in different directions

## Resume conventions for this profession
- One or two pages; for each role, event types, sizes, and budgets from your own records, the software you used, and the scope you owned; a short portfolio of past events (photos, agendas, programs) often helps
- "Detail-oriented event professional" reads as filler; "planned a yearly members' conference end to end, from venue contract to on-site registration, with a small volunteer team" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a phone screen, then an interview with the events or marketing lead, sometimes a panel; some employers ask for a sample event plan, budget, or timeline as an exercise
- Expect questions on a crisis on event day, vendor negotiation, managing a budget, and how you handle long hours around events

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $61,160 a year ($29.41 an hour); most earn between $36,830 and $101,700 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 13-1121). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 16,800 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 5.8% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 13-1121). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
- **Self-employed:** about 10% of people in this work are self-employed, and the pay figures above leave them out (BLS Projections 2025–35).
<!-- facts:end -->
- Staff planners are commonly paid an annual salary; coordinators, assistants, and venue event staff are more often hourly; some venue and hotel sales roles add a bonus or commission on bookings
- Hourly planners usually earn overtime under federal and state law for long event weeks, while salaried planners may be exempt; ask how the role is classified and whether comp time is given after events
- Shift differentials are rare, but ask about event-day pay, travel pay, and per diem; hourly event staff at venues may not have guaranteed hours outside busy seasons
- Many planners are self-employed and charge a flat fee, a percentage of the event budget, or an hourly rate; compare freelance income after taxes, insurance, and slow seasons against a salaried role with benefits

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open: base salary or hourly rate, title, travel policy and per diem, comp time after big events, remote days between events, and professional development such as CMP fees
- For freelance work, the fee structure, deposit and payment schedule, and cancellation terms tend to be negotiable; ask for them in a written contract

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: "event planner" roles that are mostly admin, heavy travel with no per diem or comp time, unpaid "trial" events, unclear budget authority, and freelance offers paid only after the event with no deposit

## Verify before relying on this
- The Events Industry Council for the CMP; the credentialing body's own site for the CSEP; your state and local rules for alcohol service, permits, and business licences if you work on your own

- Event planning isn't a regulated profession, but event permits and special-occasion liquor licences are set by provinces and municipalities, and self-employed planners may need to register for GST or HST with the CRA once revenue passes the small-supplier threshold; check your province and municipality for permit rules.
