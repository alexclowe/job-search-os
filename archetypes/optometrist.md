# Optometrist — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `optometrist` · **Aliases:** OD
**Also read:** `medical-assistant`, `physician`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Optometrist, Doctor of Optometry (OD), Associate Optometrist, Therapeutic Optometrist, and Clinic Director
- Adjacent worth searching: Optometrist in an ophthalmology practice (co-management), Pediatric or Low Vision Optometrist, Contact Lens Specialist, Residency-trained roles in veterans or community health, and Industry roles in eyecare companies

## Where the postings live
- Usually private and group optometry practices, ophthalmology practices, retail optical settings, investor-backed eyecare groups, and community health; school and state association boards carry many roles
- Some retail settings lease space to independent optometrists rather than hiring them, and fill-in (per diem) work is often arranged through networks and recruiter lists

## What a recruiter screens for first
- A Doctor of Optometry degree from an accredited school, passed exams from the National Board of Examiners in Optometry (NBEO), and a state licence; states differ in which medications and procedures optometrists may use, so recruiters often check your therapeutic certification
- The practice model you want (medical, primary care, specialty contact lenses, retail) and the equipment and electronic health record you know
- Residency training can matter for medical or hospital roles

## Story types that land
- A disease you caught early (glaucoma, diabetic eye disease, a detached retina) and the referral that followed
- Growing a specialty service such as myopia control, dry eye, or specialty contact lenses
- Keeping a busy schedule on time while keeping exam quality high
- Working with an ophthalmologist on co-managed patients

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Degree, residency, state licences, and therapeutic certification near the top; each role names the practice model and typical patient volume
- "Passionate about vision" reads as filler; "full-scope primary care optometry, managed glaucoma suspects and co-managed cataract surgery patients" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a conversation with the practice owner or regional director, then a shadow or working day; some practices ask about your approach to optical sales and medical billing
- Questions commonly cover patient volume, how you handle medical eye problems, and your interest in partnership

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $136,570 a year ($65.66 an hour); most earn between $74,870 and $202,180 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 29-1041). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 2,000 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 9.5% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a doctoral or professional degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 29-1041). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually an annual salary for employed optometrists; production-based pay (a share of professional fees you generate) is common at some employers, either alone or on top of a base
- Independent optometrists leasing space in retail settings earn from their own practice, so pay depends on volume, fees set, and lease terms

## What's usually negotiable
- Base salary or daily rate, the production percentage, sign-on bonus, continuing education money, licence and dues, schedule, and a path to partnership tend to move
- Ask about non-compete terms, patient volume per day, and whether optical sales count toward any bonus

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: high patient-per-day expectations with little technician support, production pay that excludes medical visits, pressure to push optical sales, and long non-compete terms

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state board of optometry and the Association of Regulatory Boards of Optometry (arbo.org) for licensure and scope; NBEO (optometry.org) for exam rules; your contract for pay and non-compete terms

## In Canada
