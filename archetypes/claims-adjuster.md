# Claims Adjuster / Examiner — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `claims-adjuster` · **Aliases:** claims examiner, claims representative, claims processor
**Also read:** `insurance-sales-agent`, `customer-service-rep`, `paralegal`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Claims Adjuster, Claims Examiner, Claims Representative, Claims Specialist, Claims Analyst, Field Claims Adjuster, and General Adjuster, with the line named: auto, property, casualty or liability, workers' compensation, or health and disability
- Adjacent worth searching: Desk Adjuster or Inside Claims Representative (phone and file-based), Litigation Claims Representative, Catastrophe (CAT) Adjuster, Claims Processor at a health plan or third-party administrator, Subrogation Specialist, and Insurance Underwriter

## Where the postings live
- Usually insurer and third-party administrator career pages and the general job boards; many carriers run trainee or claims academy programs for new graduates
- Independent adjusting firms often keep a roster of licensed adjusters and call them out when a storm or other catastrophe hits, so signing up with several firms may matter more than answering one posting
- Health and workers' compensation claims roles are commonly posted by health plans, self-insured employers, and administrators rather than property and casualty carriers

## What a recruiter screens for first
- An adjuster licence for the state and line, or willingness to get one: many states license adjusters through a pre-licensing course and a state exam, and people in states that don't license adjusters often get a "designated home state" licence so other states will grant a non-resident licence; staff adjusters employed by an insurer don't need a licence in many states (the NAIC notes that "only 15 states require company adjusters to be licensed"), so check the rule for your state
- Public adjusters (who work for the policyholder, not the insurer) hold a separate licence in many states
- The line and claim type you've handled, your settlement authority level, file load, and comfort with the claims system and estimating software named in the posting; for field roles, a clean driving record and willingness to travel or deploy to catastrophes

## Story types that land
- A coverage question you worked through: what the policy said, what the facts showed, and how you explained the decision to the claimant
- A file you closed fairly and on time under a heavy caseload, including keeping the claimant informed
- Spotting a sign of possible fraud or a subrogation opportunity and following it through the proper channel
- Calming an upset claimant after a loss while still holding to the policy

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Licences with states and lines near the top, then any designations; for each role, the line, claim type, typical file load, and settlement authority, taken from your own record
- "Handled a high volume of claims" reads as filler; "desk adjuster for auto physical damage, carried a caseload set by the team, cleared files within the service standard" reads as credible once you add your own figures

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter screen, then an interview with a claims supervisor built around scenarios: a claimant disputing a denial, a coverage gap, or a possible fraud red flag
- Some carriers add a writing exercise (a coverage letter or file note), a typing or systems test, or a group assessment for trainee programs

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $78,000 a year ($37.50 an hour); most earn between $48,850 and $117,040 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 13-1031, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 38,400 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 2 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a high school diploma or equivalent, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 13-1031). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Staff adjusters and examiners usually earn an annual salary, while desk and processing roles are often hourly; bonuses tied to quality or file closure are sometimes added
- Many desk and processing roles are classed as non-exempt, so overtime pay applies to extra hours; field and senior roles are more often salaried and exempt, so ask which classification the role has and how catastrophe deployments are paid
- Independent adjusters are commonly paid per claim on the firm's fee schedule (or a daily rate on some deployments), and catastrophe work usually comes with no guaranteed hours or claim volume, so income can swing with the storm season
- Shift differentials tend to be uncommon outside claims call centres that run evening or weekend first-notice-of-loss lines

## What's usually negotiable
- For staff roles, the salary or hourly rate within the band, the level (trainee, adjuster, senior), a company car or mileage for field work, and remote days tend to move
- For independent work, the split with the firm, per diem and travel, and whether the firm pays licence and certification costs are often worth asking about

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: "unlimited earning potential" with no fee schedule shown, fees to join a roster or buy training, a caseload the posting leaves out, and "trainee" roles that expect you to carry a full caseload from the start

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state department of insurance and the National Insurance Producer Registry (NIPR) for adjuster licence rules and non-resident licensing; the NAIC for model licensing guidance; the firm's fee schedule and contract for independent pay

## In Canada
<!-- canada:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **NOC 2021:** 12201, matched by title by the maintainers (not an official concordance; check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site).
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- Adjuster licensing is provincial: in Ontario, independent and public adjusters are licensed by FSRA and must be sponsored by a licensed adjusting firm at all times, while adjusters employed by a licensed insurer usually don't need their own licence, and in Quebec claims adjusters hold a certificate from the AMF, or in some cases work under a certified adjuster's supervision while qualifying; rules differ elsewhere, so check your provincial insurance regulator.
