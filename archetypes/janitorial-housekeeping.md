# Janitor / Housekeeper — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `janitorial-housekeeping` · **Aliases:** custodian, housekeeper, cleaner
**Also read:** `maintenance-technician`, `property-manager`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Janitor, Custodian, Cleaner, Housekeeper or Room Attendant, Environmental Services (EVS) Technician in hospitals, Building Services Worker, School Custodian, and Lead Custodian
- Adjacent worth searching: Porter or Day Porter, Floor Technician, Laundry Attendant, Housekeeping Supervisor, and Facilities or Maintenance Helper

## Where the postings live
- Usually the general job boards, contract cleaning companies, hotels, hospitals, schools and school districts, universities, and local government job sites; many contract cleaners hire continuously through quick-apply forms
- Public-sector custodian jobs commonly go through a formal application on the government or school district site, while hotels and cleaning companies may hire at walk-in events or through referrals from current staff

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Availability, often evenings, overnight, or early mornings, plus reliable transport to the site and whether you can do the physical work in the posting
- Experience with cleaning chemicals and safety data sheets, floor machines, and for hospitals, infection-control cleaning; many employers run a background check, especially in schools and healthcare

## Story types that land
- Keeping a large area or building clean on a tight schedule, and how you organized the route
- Handling a spill, biohazard, or urgent cleanup safely and quickly
- Passing an inspection or getting good guest or patient feedback
- Noticing and reporting a safety or maintenance problem before it got worse

## Resume conventions for this profession
- One page or the online form alone; list each job with the building type and size, the equipment you used, and any training (chemical safety, bloodborne pathogens, floor care)
- "Detail-oriented" reads as filler; "cleaned a three-floor office building each night, stripped and waxed hallways, trained two new cleaners" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a short interview with a supervisor or hiring manager, focused on availability, reliability, and past cleaning experience
- Some employers add a walk-through of the building, a background check, and a drug screen; public-sector jobs may use a structured panel or a scored application

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $17.71 an hour ($36,840 a year); most earn between $13.77 and $24.17 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 37-2011, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 497,300 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 2 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** no formal credential, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 37-2011). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly, with a shift differential for nights or overnights at many employers, and sometimes extra pay for lead or specialized work
- Janitors and housekeepers are usually non-exempt, so overtime pay applies after a full workweek under federal law; ask how extra shifts and call-ins are paid
- Guaranteed hours vary: contract cleaning and hotel housekeeping often have part-time or variable schedules, so ask about minimum hours per week and how hours change in slow seasons
- Unionized building services, hotels, hospitals, and public employers commonly publish wage steps in the contract or pay schedule

## What's usually negotiable
- The starting rate often moves little at entry level, but the shift, the number of hours, and a lead or specialized role (floor care, healthcare cleaning) may be open to discussion
- Where a union scale or public pay schedule exists, the step you start on usually moves more than the rate itself

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: "independent contractor" cleaning work paid by the job with no employee protections, room or square-footage quotas that seem unrealistic, split shifts with no extra pay, and no mention of safety training

## Verify before relying on this
- The Department of Labor's overtime pages and your state labor department for overtime and scheduling rules; OSHA's pages for chemical safety and bloodborne pathogen rules; the posting and offer letter for shift, rate, and differentials
