# Financial Advisor — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `financial-advisor` · **Aliases:** wealth advisor, financial planner, securities/registered representative
**Also read:** `insurance-sales-agent`, `financial-analyst`, `loan-officer`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Financial Advisor, Financial Planner, Wealth Advisor, Wealth Manager, Financial Consultant, Investment Advisor, and Registered Representative, with the junior versions (Associate Advisor, Paraplanner, Client Service Associate)
- Adjacent worth searching: Portfolio Manager, Private Banker, Relationship Manager, Retirement Plan Consultant, Financial Counselor, Bank-Based Advisor, and Financial Life Planner

## Where the postings live
- Usually firm career pages, the general job boards, and professional networks; large brokerage firms, insurance companies, and banks often recruit continuously for trainee advisors
- Independent and fee-only firms tend to hire through referrals, planning associations, and job boards run by financial-planning groups, and experienced advisors with a book of clients are commonly recruited directly by other firms

## What a recruiter screens for first
- The licences the role needs, or willingness to get them. In the US that often means FINRA (the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority) exams: the SIE (Securities Industry Essentials), which can often be taken before you're hired, plus the Series 7, which needs a sponsoring firm; then state exams such as the Series 63, or the Series 65 (or the Series 66, which counts only when paired with the Series 7) for giving investment advice for a fee; many advisors also hold a state insurance licence to sell life insurance and annuities
- The CFP (Certified Financial Planner) is a voluntary credential from CFP Board with education, exam, experience, and ethics steps; it's often preferred, especially at planning-focused firms
- For experienced advisors, the size and makeup of the clients you serve, and whether they're likely to follow you to a new firm

## Story types that land
- Building a plan that changed a client's outcome (retirement, college, a business sale) in plain words
- Keeping a client invested and calm through a market drop
- Growing your client base through referrals or a niche you understand well
- Catching a risk the client hadn't seen, such as missing insurance or a tax problem

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Licences and credentials near the top (the Series exams held, state insurance lines, CFP or progress toward it); each role names the client type, the kind of work (planning, investments, insurance), and results you can confirm, such as clients served or assets you managed
- "Top producer" and "trusted advisor" read as filler without a figure from your own record; mark any number you can't confirm and fill it in before sending, and be careful that any performance claim follows your firm's compliance rules

## How interviews usually run
- Often a phone screen, then interviews with a branch or market manager; trainee programs may include an aptitude test, a business plan for finding your first clients, and a list of people you know
- Experienced advisors usually discuss their client base, how they work, and the transition package, and planning firms may ask you to walk through a case

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $78,660 a year ($37.82 an hour); most earn between $48,040 and $212,880 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 41-3031, the largest of the occupations this archetype covers). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 52,200 a year projected for 2025–35 across the 2 occupations it covers (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a bachelor's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 41-3031). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Pay tends to follow one of three models: fee-based (a percentage of assets managed, a flat planning fee, or an hourly fee), commission on products sold, or a salary plus bonus, common at banks and planning firms; many advisors earn a mix
- Trainee programs commonly pay a salary or a draw for a set period, then shift to production-based pay; ask whether any draw is recoverable (paid back from later commission) or non-recoverable
- Some products pay ongoing trail commissions or renewal commissions, and fee-based pay recurs while clients stay; ask how these are split with the firm
- Commissions on insurance and some investments can be charged back if a policy lapses or an investment is redeemed early, and transition loans or packages from a new firm commonly have clawback terms if you leave before the term ends

## What's usually negotiable
- Often open to discussion: the payout rate or grid, the base or draw and how long it lasts, transition packages for experienced advisors, support staff, licensing costs, and marketing support
- Ask who owns the client book if you leave, what non-solicit or protocol rules apply, and whether any deferred pay is forfeited on leaving, and get the answers in writing

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: pressure to sell first to friends and family, commission-only trainee roles with no salary or draw, fees to join or to buy leads, "unlimited income" with no typical figures, and heavy recruiting language about building a team below you

## Verify before relying on this
- FINRA's qualification exam pages and BrokerCheck, NASAA (the North American Securities Administrators Association) and your state securities regulator for the Series 63, 65, and 66; your state department of insurance for insurance licensing; CFP Board for CFP requirements; the firm's agreement for payout, draw, and book-ownership terms

## In Canada
<!-- canada:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **NOC 2021:** 11102; 11103, matched by title by the maintainers (not an official concordance; check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site).
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- Selling securities or mutual funds usually means registering through CIRO (the Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization) and your provincial securities regulator, financial planning credentials in Canada commonly come through FP Canada, and selling life insurance usually needs the LLQP; in Ontario, FSRA controls who may use the financial planner and financial advisor titles. Check your provincial securities regulator and FSRA's list of approved credentials before using either title.
