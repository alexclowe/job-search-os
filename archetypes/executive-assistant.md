# Executive Assistant — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `executive-assistant` · **Aliases:** EA, executive administrative assistant, chief of staff
**Also read:** `administrative-assistant`, `office-manager`, `event-planner`, `project-manager`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Executive Assistant, Executive Administrative Assistant, Executive Secretary, Senior Executive Assistant, and Executive Assistant to the CEO (Chief Executive Officer) or another named leader
- Adjacent worth searching: Administrative Coordinator, Chief of Staff (usually a step up with more strategy and project ownership), Executive Business Partner, Office Manager, Board Liaison, and Virtual Executive Assistant

## Where the postings live
- Usually company career pages, the general job boards, and professional networks; many senior executive assistant roles are filled through specialist administrative recruiters or by referral from other assistants
- Some roles often go unposted because an executive brings an assistant with them or asks their network, so staying in touch with past executives and assistant communities often pays off

## What a recruiter screens for first
- The level of the people you supported (CEO, senior leaders, board), how many you supported at once, and the calendar, travel, and meeting load you handled
- Tools such as Outlook or Google Workspace, SharePoint, Zoom or Teams, and an expense tool such as SAP Concur; discretion with confidential information; no licence is usually needed, though some assistants hold a voluntary credential such as the IAAP (International Association of Administrative Professionals) Certified Administrative Professional

## Story types that land
- Untangling a chaotic calendar or travel crisis and protecting the executive's time
- Handling something confidential with discretion (a reorganization, a board matter, a personnel issue)
- Running a board meeting, offsite, or event from start to finish
- Acting as the executive's gatekeeper and representative: deciding what reaches them and following up for them

## Resume conventions for this profession
- One to two pages; a short summary naming the level of executives supported, then each role with who you supported, the scope (calendar, travel, board, projects), and the tools you used
- "Right hand to the CEO" reads as filler on its own; "supported the CEO and two senior leaders across three time zones, prepared quarterly board materials" reads as credible when it's yours; mark any figure you can't confirm and fill it in before sending

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter screen, an interview with the current or outgoing assistant or HR, then a meeting with the executive; chemistry with the executive usually decides the final round
- Many employers use a scheduling puzzle, a prioritization exercise ("these five requests arrive at once"), an email-drafting task, or a travel-planning test, and questions about confidentiality and handling pressure are usual

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $76,590 a year ($36.82 an hour); most earn between $50,560 and $109,850 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 43-6011). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 43,300 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 0.0% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a high school diploma or equivalent, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 43-6011). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Either an annual salary or hourly pay; many executive assistant roles are classed as non-exempt, so they may earn overtime for hours over the weekly limit, while senior roles are more often salaried and exempt
- Some employers add an annual bonus, especially in finance, law, and large companies; on-call or after-hours expectations for a demanding executive may or may not come with extra pay
- Part-time, contract, or virtual assistant work often varies in hours, so ask about guaranteed hours each week

## What's usually negotiable
- Salary or hourly rate, exempt or non-exempt status (which decides overtime), bonus, title (for example senior executive assistant or chief of staff), remote days, and training support may move
- Ask how after-hours and travel expectations are handled; extra pay, comp time, or a higher base sometimes follows when the executive's schedule is heavy

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: supporting many executives at once at a single-assistant salary, "available 24/7" or "flexible hours" with no mention of extra pay, personal errands for the executive's family listed as core duties, and a chief of staff title with purely administrative work

## Verify before relying on this
- The US Department of Labor and your state labor department for exempt-status and overtime rules; the employer's offer letter for bonus and after-hours terms; the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook for how the field is changing
