# Carpenter — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `carpenter` · **Aliases:** finish carpenter, framer
**Also read:** `construction-manager`, `electrician`, `plumber`, `maintenance-technician`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Apprentice Carpenter, Carpenter Helper, Journeyman Carpenter, Rough or Framing Carpenter, Finish or Trim Carpenter, Form Carpenter, Commercial or Interior Systems Carpenter (metal studs, drywall, ceilings), and Lead Carpenter or Carpenter Foreman
- Adjacent worth searching: Maintenance Carpenter for campuses and hospitals, Cabinet Installer or Cabinetmaker, Scaffold Builder, Remodeling or Renovation Carpenter, Superintendent or Assistant Superintendent, and Estimator

## Where the postings live
- Usually general contractor, framing, and remodeling company sites, the general job boards, and facilities career pages; union carpenters are commonly dispatched through the local union and its apprenticeship program
- Many small crews hire by word of mouth and through suppliers or other trades, so telling people you know on job sites often surfaces work before it is posted

## What a recruiter screens for first
- The kind of carpentry you have done (framing, forms, finish, commercial interiors), reading prints and layout, and the tools you own; a driver's licence and reliable transport to changing job sites are commonly screened
- Safety training such as an OSHA (Occupational Safety and Health Administration) 10- or 30-hour card (a course completion card, not a certification), fall protection, and scaffold or lift training; in the US, licensing tends to apply to contractors who run jobs rather than to carpenters on a crew, but some states and cities licence certain work, so check yours
- The knockout questions: your own tools, start date, and whether you can handle the physical demands and weather

## Story types that land
- A structure or finish you built from prints, and a layout problem you caught before it cost time
- Working fast and clean on a tight schedule while keeping quality up
- A safety habit or a hazard you fixed on a crowded site
- Leading a small crew, training an apprentice, or working around other trades

## Resume conventions for this profession
- One page is usually enough: a skills line (framing, forms, finish, layout, tools and equipment), then safety cards, then each job with the project type and your part in it; photos of your work can help for finish and remodeling roles
- "Skilled craftsman" reads as filler; "framed and sheathed custom homes from prints, set trusses, ran layout for a crew of four" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often an informal talk with the foreman, superintendent, or owner, sometimes on the job site; some employers add a paid trial day to see your pace and skill
- Expect questions on the work you have done, the tools you own, reading prints, and how you work safely at height

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $29.12 an hour ($60,580 a year); most earn between $19.43 and $48.03 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 47-2031). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 62,800 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 3.9% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a high school diploma or equivalent, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 47-2031). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
- **Self-employed:** about 25% of people in this work are self-employed, and the pay figures above leave them out (BLS Projections 2025–35).
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly: union carpenters tend to be paid on the local's published scale with benefit and pension contributions, and apprentices earn a rising share of the journeyman rate; non-union and small-crew pay is commonly set by the contractor
- Overtime rules usually follow federal and state law, and long days are common near deadlines; shift differentials are uncommon outside industrial or night work, and guaranteed hours are rare on construction, where rain days and gaps between jobs can cut the week short
- Many carpenters work for themselves at some point, and self-employed or piece-rate work often comes with no overtime or benefits, so compare total yearly earnings rather than the rate alone

## What's usually negotiable
- At non-union and small shops the hourly rate, a tool allowance, a company vehicle or mileage, paid training, and year-round work may move; union scale usually doesn't, though classification can
- Ask how the crew's hours looked through the last winter or slow season

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: being paid as a "subcontractor" while working set hours for one boss (possible misclassification), cash pay with no records, no mention of fall protection, and pay "depending on experience" with no range

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state or city licensing office for any trade or contractor licence (CareerOneStop's License Finder lists them; link, don't copy); the Department of Labor's apprenticeship site for registered programs; the Department of Labor's guidance on employee versus contractor status
