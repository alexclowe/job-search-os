# Pharmacy Technician — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `pharmacy-technician` · **Aliases:** CPhT
**Also read:** `medical-assistant`, `medical-admin`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Pharmacy Technician, Certified Pharmacy Technician (CPhT), Pharmacy Tech Trainee, Lead or Senior Pharmacy Technician, and Inpatient or Hospital Pharmacy Technician
- Adjacent worth searching: IV (intravenous) or Sterile Compounding Technician, Chemotherapy Pharmacy Technician, Medication History Technician, Prior Authorization Specialist, Pharmacy Buyer, and Mail-Order or Specialty Pharmacy Technician

## Where the postings live
- Usually retail and grocery pharmacies, hospital and health-system pharmacies, mail-order and specialty pharmacies, and long-term care pharmacies; health-system career sites and the general boards carry most of them
- Retail chains often hire trainees continuously and may pay for certification; hospital roles more often ask for certification and prior experience up front

## What a recruiter screens for first
- Registration or a licence with your state board of pharmacy (rules vary a lot by state; some register trainees, some expect national certification within a set time)
- National certification: the CPhT from the Pharmacy Technician Certification Board (PTCB) or from the National Healthcareer Association's Exam for the Certification of Pharmacy Technicians (ExCPT); hospital roles commonly ask for sterile compounding experience
- Accuracy, speed, the pharmacy software, and your availability for evenings, weekends, and holidays

## Story types that land
- Catching an error before it reached a patient: a wrong drug, dose, or patient
- Keeping up during a rush (flu season, a staffing gap) without trading accuracy for speed
- Solving an insurance rejection or prior authorization so a patient could get their medication
- Improving inventory, a sterile compounding process, or an audit result

## Resume conventions for this profession
- State registration or licence and national certification near the top; each job names the setting (retail, hospital, mail order) and the work (filling, compounding, insurance, inventory)
- "Strong attention to detail" reads as filler; "filled and checked high-volume retail prescriptions, resolved insurance rejections, and handled controlled-substance counts" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a phone screen, then an interview with the pharmacy manager or pharmacist in charge; hospital roles may add a math or calculation test and a sterile compounding skills check
- Questions commonly cover handling an upset customer, what you do if you suspect an error, and your availability

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $22.00 an hour ($45,750 a year); most earn between $17.32 and $29.35 an hour (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 29-2052). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 44,700 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 6.4% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a high school diploma or equivalent, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 29-2052). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually hourly, with overtime for extra hours; evening, night, weekend, and holiday differentials are common in hospitals and some retail stores
- Ask about guaranteed hours: retail technician hours can depend on the store's labor budget and prescription volume
- Some employers add pay for certification, sterile compounding, or lead duties; unionized hospitals often publish wage steps in the contract

## What's usually negotiable
- The starting rate within the range (often by certification and years of experience), certification or compounding pay, schedule, and differentials tend to move
- Paid certification exams, training toward sterile compounding, and tuition help toward pharmacy school are often worth asking about

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: one technician covering a busy store alone, "flexible scheduling" with no minimum hours, duties that sound like the pharmacist's job, and metrics-heavy language with no mention of staffing

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state board of pharmacy for registration, licensure, and certification rules (the National Association of Boards of Pharmacy lists them); PTCB and NHA for certification rules; CareerOneStop's License Finder for your state

## In Canada
<!-- canada:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **NOC 2021:** 32124; 33103, matched by title by the maintainers (not an official concordance; check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site).
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- Pharmacy technician is a regulated title in most provinces, usually after the PEBC qualifying exam (from the Pharmacy Examining Board of Canada) and registration with the provincial pharmacy regulator, while pharmacy assistant is a separate, unregulated role; check your provincial pharmacy regulator before relying on a US certification.
