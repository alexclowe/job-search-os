# Nurse Practitioner — job-search archetype

<!-- uses: onet -->

**Slug:** `nurse-practitioner` · **Aliases:** NP, FNP, PMHNP
**Also read:** `nurse`, `licensed-practical-nurse`, `physician`

## Target titles (and the adjacent ones)
- Nurse Practitioner with the population named: Family (FNP), Adult-Gerontology Primary or Acute Care (AGPCNP, AGACNP), Pediatric (PNP), Psychiatric-Mental Health (PMHNP), Women's Health (WHNP), and Neonatal (NNP); also Advanced Practice Registered Nurse (APRN) and Advanced Practice Provider (APP)
- Adjacent worth searching: Hospitalist NP, Urgent Care or Retail Clinic Provider, Telehealth Provider, Lead APP or APP Manager, Clinical Instructor or NP faculty, and roles posted as "provider" that accept an NP or a physician assistant

## Where the postings live
- Usually health-system career sites, physician groups, urgent care and retail clinic operators, telehealth companies, community health centers, and the general boards
- Many NP roles are filled through physician recruiters, clinical rotation contacts, and NP associations' job boards; locum tenens agencies commonly place short-term contracts

## What a recruiter screens for first
- An active RN licence, a graduate degree (master's or doctorate) from an NP program, national board certification in the population the job serves, and a state APRN or NP licence; recruiters commonly check that your certification matches the role (a family NP for primary care, a psychiatric NP for mental health)
- National certification usually comes from the American Academy of Nurse Practitioners Certification Board (now operating as NPCB), the American Nurses Credentialing Center (ANCC), or a specialty board; prescribing controlled substances also needs a federal DEA (Drug Enforcement Administration) registration and sometimes a state one
- Practice authority differs by state: some let NPs practice independently, others ask for a collaborating or supervising physician, so employers often screen for which state licences you hold and how soon you could be credentialed

## Story types that land
- A diagnosis you caught or a patient you escalated in time, told with the clinical reasoning
- Managing a full panel or a busy shift: how you balanced visit volume with careful care and documentation
- Improving an outcome for a group of patients (diabetes control, readmissions, screening rates), stated only as what you actually measured
- Working with physicians, nurses, and staff to fix a workflow or handoff problem

## Resume conventions for this profession
- Credentials line near the top (degree, certification, state licences, DEA registration); for new graduates, clinical rotations listed with setting, specialty, and hours in the format the employer asks for
- Each role names the setting, the patient population, and typical daily visit volume or census; prior RN experience stays on the resume, briefly
- "Provided holistic care" reads as filler; "saw adult primary care patients in a community health center, managed chronic disease panels, and precepted NP students" reads as credible

## How interviews usually run
- Often a recruiter screen, then interviews with the medical director, lead APP, and practice manager; many include a clinical case discussion or a shadow day
- Common topics tend to include how you handle a patient beyond your comfort level, your documentation and coding approach, visit volume expectations, and your collaboration with physicians

## How pay is usually structured
<!-- facts:start (generated from the evidence pack by scripts/job-search-os/render-archetype-facts.py; do not edit by hand) -->
- **US pay, for comparison:** median $132,300 a year ($63.61 an hour); most earn between $101,340 and $174,420 a year (BLS OEWS May 2025, SOC 29-1171). Ten percent earn less than the low figure and ten percent more than the high one.
- **What those pay figures cover:** employees only, not the self-employed; they include commissions, production bonuses and tips, and leave out overtime, shift differentials and non-production bonuses (BLS OEWS technical note).
- **Openings:** about 29,400 a year projected for 2025–35; employment projected to grow 41.0% over the decade (BLS Projections 2025–35).
- **Typical entry education:** a master's degree, the level BLS lists as typical for entry (BLS Projections 2025–35, SOC 29-1171). Individual employers may ask for more or less.
<!-- facts:end -->
- Usually an annual salary; some roles, especially in hospitals and urgent care, pay hourly or per shift instead
- A productivity bonus is common at some employers, often based on visits or work RVUs (relative value units, a billing-based measure of work); ask how it is calculated and whether the target is realistic for the schedule
- Sign-on bonuses, a continuing education allowance, malpractice coverage (including tail coverage if you leave), and paid time for administrative work are part of the total package

## What's usually negotiable
- Base salary, the productivity bonus formula and threshold, sign-on and relocation bonuses, continuing education money and days, and schedule tend to move
- Ask about malpractice tail coverage, non-compete terms, the patient-per-day expectation, and how long you have to ramp up before productivity targets start

## Red flags in postings
- Often worth a second look: a high patient-per-day target with no ramp-up for new graduates, "independent" roles with no physician backup in a state that asks for collaboration, broad non-compete clauses, and pay that is mostly productivity bonus with a low base

## Verify before relying on this
- Your state board of nursing for APRN licensure and practice authority; the AANP's state practice environment map (aanp.org); NPCB (formerly AANPCB) and ANCC for certification rules; the DEA for controlled-substance registration

## In Canada

- **NOC 2021:** 31302, matched by title by the maintainers (not an official concordance; check the unit group's definition on Statistics Canada's NOC site).
- **Outlook and wages:** Job Bank publishes a three-year outlook and wage ranges by NOC and region; check your province there rather than using US figures.
<!-- canada:end -->
- Nurse practitioners are regulated by each province's or territory's nursing regulator, and scope and prescribing rules differ by province; a US nurse practitioner licence or certification doesn't carry over directly, so check your provincial nursing regulator first.
