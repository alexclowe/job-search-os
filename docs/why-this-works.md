# Why This Works

The thinking behind the Job Search AI Operating System — why it's built around five jobs and a weekly rhythm, why the minimum pay comes first, why every claim has to trace to your own record, and what it deliberately leaves to you. Optional reading; nothing here is needed to run a job.

---

## Two ways job seekers fail with AI

People who bring AI to a job search usually hit one of two walls. Either they use it as a vending machine — "write me a cover letter" — and get something that reads like every other AI cover letter, which in 2026 is exactly what screening tools and hiring managers have learned to spot. Or they let it be generous on their behalf: a resume that says "led" where they contributed, a percentage nobody measured, a title that's a half-step up. It reads well right up to the interview question "what exactly did you do?"

The Operating System is built to avoid both.

**The first fix is the profile.** Setup captures your career story once — what you're aiming for, the number you won't go below, how you're paid, the thing about your record you'd rather get ahead of, and the work you're proudest of with what came of it. Every job and every skill reads it. You never re-explain who you are, and nothing is written from a blank page.

**The second fix is the record.** Every metric, title, date, and accomplishment in any output has to trace to your resume, your profile, or your Story Bank. Anything that doesn't becomes `[confirm]`. A guard checks every resume, letter, and answer for claims that outrun the record and for the words that mark a document as machine-written. The result is material you can defend in the room — which is the only kind worth sending.

**The third fix is the rhythm.** A search runs on a weekly cadence or it runs on anxiety. The five jobs map onto what a search actually consists of: apply to a posting, work the inbox, prepare for the round that's next, follow up and negotiate, and plan the week. The command center shows them every time you open it, so you don't have to decide what to do — you pick.

## Why your minimum pay comes first

The most expensive mistake in a job search isn't a bad interview. It's three rounds with a company that was never going to pay what you need. It costs a week of evenings, and it costs the energy that should have gone to the right process.

So the salary check runs before anything else. Paste a posting, and the first line you see is whether its pay clears your minimum, compared like with like: an hourly rate against your hourly minimum, a salary against your salary minimum, a commission role's base and realistic expected total each against the matching number, a salary-schedule placement against the step you need. Differentials and "up to" commission figures are never counted as base. A recruiter's message with a number gets the same check. An offer's first line is the same comparison. The flag is plain — "at or below your minimum pay, flagging it now, not after three rounds" — and it never soft-pedals. You can still apply; it just won't let a role below your minimum become your main option by accident.

This is the product's one opinion: fewer, better, and never below your minimum pay. It will swap an "apply to twenty more" plan for one positioning move, and it will tell you when replies are thin because the story isn't landing, not because you haven't applied enough.

## Why what you did comes before titles

Titles compress badly. "Staff nurse" says nothing about the new graduates you precepted; "teacher" hides the reading program you rebuilt; "engineering manager" says nothing about whether you still do the hands-on work. Most of what makes a hiring manager hesitate — a gap, a career change, all your experience in one setting, a manager title on hands-on work, no big-name employer — is a title problem.

Every output in the pack leads with what you did — built, fixed, ran, improved, taught, or cared for, and what changed as a result. That's what a hiring manager is trying to find out anyway. It's also what answers the hesitation before anyone voices it: you don't explain that you can do the new kind of work, you show the time you already did it.

The Story Bank is where this lives. Six to ten stories, each with the situation, what you did, and what changed, written once. Interview prep maps them to the round's likely worries. A behavioral question gets answered from one. A reference gets briefed on the two they witnessed. Your resume summary and your LinkedIn About say the same thing. Consistency is what a hiring panel's debrief rewards.

## What a job does and doesn't do

Each job runs a whole piece of work, start to finish, and puts the results where you already work:

- **Files** for what leaves your hands — the resume and cover letter as Word documents, the offer worksheet as a spreadsheet with formulas you can check.
- **Living trackers** for what you come back to — the Application Board, the Story Bank, the Offer Tracker, the Weekly Search Log — as artifacts in your sidebar that you can edit in place, with a CSV backup in your Project.
- **Drafts** for anything that goes to a person — in your Gmail Drafts folder when Gmail is connected, paste-ready otherwise.

What no job does: send, submit, accept, decline, resign, or give notice. Reading your inbox means finding recruiter and hiring-team messages and showing you the list to confirm. Connected tools are personal accounts only; a search never runs through an employer's systems. Scheduled runs prepare and wait.

## What it leaves to you

The system is careful about the line between structure and judgment.

- **Your minimum pay** — it holds to the number; you set it, and you can change it.
- **Whether to take an offer** — the decision memo lays the terms against your must-haves and names the thing to sleep on. The yes is yours.
- **Anything with legal weight** — separation agreements, equity treatment, non-competes, notice periods. The severance and negotiation skills help you ask the right questions and flag every statutory point for a professional; they don't answer them.
- **What you say about why you're leaving** — the one line you approved in setup, and nothing beyond it.
- **Whether a claim is true** — it can check that a claim traces to your record. Only you can confirm it happened the way it's written.

## What changes when it's working

- A posting takes fifteen minutes to evaluate and apply to, not an evening — and the ones that don't clear your minimum pay take one minute.
- The inbox stops being where processes die. Every recruiter gets a reply that asks the right questions; every scheduling thread ends up on the calendar; every rejection from a human teaches one thing and is then closed.
- Interview prep starts from your stories, not from a question bank, and the story is the same in every round.
- The offer conversation happens with a worksheet in front of you and the exact words written down, with your minimum pay in the first line.
- Monday has three moves with time protected for them, and a log that shows, four weeks in, whether the funnel is moving.

That's the whole idea: the structure of a search is repetitive; the judgment about what to pursue isn't. The system handles the structure. You bring the judgment.
